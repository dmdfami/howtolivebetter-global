# 34. Keep household medicine from causing harm

Fever reducers, painkillers, cold medicines, stomach medicines and anti-diarrhea pills are mostly sold at pharmacies without a prescription. Buying without a prescription does not mean taking them any way you like. This section does not say which medicine to take for each illness. It picks out a few things where one action avoids serious harm.

All entries in this section are judged by mortality. Most look at serious outcomes such as liver failure, stomach bleeding and kidney failure in newborns. A few look at headaches and reactions while driving. The benefit is mostly "one fewer serious accident", and the research cannot give a figure for how much less often it happens. So the size of each benefit is judged by how serious the outcome is, and each entry's notes explain why.

Some topics are covered elsewhere, so this section only points to them. For what to do after accidentally swallowing a medicine or a cleaning product, see section 13, item 20 (accidental ingestion). For taking chronic-disease medicine exactly as prescribed, see section 16, item 1 (take prescribed medicine in full). For buying prescription drugs online, a prescription check comes first; see section 28, item 6 (for weight-loss drugs, get a prescription from a hospital).

The people who benefit from these entries are you and your family, which places them in the top two benefit tiers.

### 1. Before taking two cold or pain medicines together, check the ingredient list: acetaminophen should appear in only one of them, and if you take too much, go to hospital the same day even if you feel fine
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=health -->
- Cost: Free. Check the ingredient list on each box; it takes a minute or two.
- Plain language: Many cold medicines contain acetaminophen, so taking two of them together means a double dose, which can harm the liver. In the US, about 27% of people with acetaminophen-caused acute liver failure died before getting a liver transplant. If you take too much, go to hospital right away, even if you feel fine, because the antidote works best when given early.
- Benefit: Over six years, 22 hospitals in the US recorded 662 cases of acute liver failure in a prospective registry. Of these, 275 cases (42%) were caused by acetaminophen, and the median amount swallowed was 24 grams. Of these 275 people, 131 (48%) took too much by accident rather than on purpose. Among the accidental overdoses, 38% were taking two or more products containing this ingredient. Of the 275, 27% died without getting a liver transplant, and 8% had a liver transplant. In 2020, China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) required OTC labels to state that overdose causes serious liver damage and that the recommended maximum daily dose is no more than 2 grams. The label must also say to avoid, as far as possible, using it together with drugs that contain acetaminophen or other antipyretic-analgesics. US rules require the first liver warning on this type of OTC label to name three liver-harming situations: going over the one-day maximum, taking it with other acetaminophen-containing drugs, and drinking more than three glasses of alcohol a day while using it. The same NMPA notice requires extended-release labels to state that adults or children who take too much must see a doctor immediately, whether or not they feel unwell. The antidote is N-acetylcysteine. Its label says to start it early: it works well within 12 hours and less well after 24 hours. A US multicenter study from 1976 to 1985 followed 2,540 overdose patients treated with this antidote. Among patients judged by blood level to be at risk of liver damage, 6.1% of those who started the antidote within 10 hours had liver injury. Among those who started between 10 and 24 hours, 26.4% had liver injury. Starting the antidote within 8 hours of an overdose protected the liver no matter how high the blood level was.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Larson AM, Polson J, Fontana RJ, et al.; Acute Liver Failure Study Group (2005). Acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure: results of a United States multicenter, prospective study. Hepatology, 42(6):1364-1372. <https://doi.org/10.1002/hep.20948>；国家药监局关于修订对乙酰氨基酚常释及缓释制剂说明书的公告（2020年第15号）及附件1《对乙酰氨基酚常释制剂处方药说明书修订要求》、附件2《对乙酰氨基酚常释制剂非处方药说明书修订要求》、附件3《对乙酰氨基酚缓释制剂说明书修订要求》（湖南省药品监督管理局转载）. <http://mpa.hunan.gov.cn/mpa/xxgk/tzgg/wjtz/202003/t20200311_11809335.html>；21 CFR 201.326(a)(1)(iii)(A) Liver warning. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>；Smilkstein MJ, Knapp GL, Kulig KW, Rumack BH (1988). Efficacy of oral N-acetylcysteine in the treatment of acetaminophen overdose: analysis of the national multicenter study (1976 to 1985). - Notes: China only: Acetaminophen is also called paracetamol. Combination cold products whose Chinese generic names include "an-fen" (the acetaminophen term) or "an-ka-huang-min" contain it. When in doubt, take all the boxes to a pharmacist. Rated B because the US figures come from a case registry with no comparison group, so the effect of checking the ingredient list cannot be calculated. The benefit is rated "large" because nearly 30% of acute liver failure cases end in death. The figure that early antidote works best comes from a non-randomized multicenter observational study that compared only against past cases.

### 2. Do not give children aspirin, analgin (metamizole) or nimesulide, do not give analgin-containing cold medicine to anyone under 18, and measure liquid fever medicine with the dosing device packed in the box
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=health who=infant,child -->
- Cost: Free. Check the generic name and ingredient list when buying a fever medicine.
- Plain language: Aspirin is linked to Reye's syndrome, a disease that damages the brain and liver and kills 31% of affected children. Nimesulide is banned for children under 12, and analgin for people under 18. Use the dosing device packed with the medicine, because household spoons vary a lot. When you switch between drops and liquid, do not give the same volume as before.
- Benefit: Reye's syndrome causes brain disease and fatty liver, usually after influenza or chickenpox. A US national surveillance program from 1981 to 1997 recorded 1,207 cases in people under 18. Salicylate, the ingredient in aspirin-type drugs, was found in the blood of 82% of these children. The overall fatality rate was 31%. Warnings on salicylate drugs began in 1980. That year saw 555 cases reported, the peak; from 1987 onward, there were no more than 36 cases a year. In 2011, China's former State Food and Drug Administration (SFDA) ruled that oral nimesulide products must not be used in children under 12. In 2020, the NMPA ruled that analgin tablets must not be used in people under 18. Compound qinghao-analgin tablets, Chonggan Ling tablets and Chonggan Ling capsules, which contain analgin, are also banned for under-18s. On dosing, look at the US figures. In 2011, the US FDA explained that infant acetaminophen drops contain 80 mg per 0.8 mL, while the oral liquid for older children contains 160 mg per 5 mL. Calculated from these numbers, 1 mL of the drops contains about three times the dose of 1 mL of the liquid. The FDA said that two concentrations on the market at once are easy to confuse and can lead to accidental overdose in children, so the dosing devices for different products must never be swapped. A Greek study measured 71 teaspoons and 49 tablespoons from 25 households. When filled with water, their volumes ranged from 2.5 mL to 7.3 mL. In a US emergency-department study, parents who measured doses in teaspoons or tablespoons made dosing errors 42.5% of the time. Parents who used only milliliters made errors 27.6% of the time. Adjusted OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.2–4.4 (the odds of a dosing error were roughly more than twice as high).
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Belay ED, Bresee JS, Holman RC, Khan AS, Shahriari A, Schonberger LB (1999). Reye's syndrome in the United States from 1981 through 1997. New England Journal of Medicine, 340(18):1377-1382. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199905063401801>；关于加强尼美舒利口服制剂使用管理的通知（国食药监安〔2011〕209号）. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/xxgk/fgwj/gzwj/gzwjyp/20110515145101552.html>；国家药监局关于修订安乃近相关品种说明书的公告（2020年第34号）及附件1至3. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/directory/web/nmpa/xxgk/ggtg/ypggtg/ypshmshxdgg/20200317171201660.html>；U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Questions and Answers – Important change in concentration for over-the-counter (OTC) liquid acetaminophen marketed for infants. <https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/questions-and-answers-important-change-concentration-over-counter-otc-liquid-acetaminophen-marketed>；Falagas ME, Vouloumanou EK, Plessa E, Peppas G, Rafailidis PI (2010). - Notes: China only: Analgin is not a first-choice drug even for adults. Its label says it can cause agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia and anaphylactic shock, so it is for short-term use only, when the illness is severe and no other effective drug is available. Chonggan Ling sounds like an ordinary cold medicine but contains analgin. Ask a pediatrician or pharmacist which fever medicine to use for a child and how much to give by weight. A baby under 3 months with a fever must go to hospital directly; see section 20, item 8 (go straight to hospital at 38 °C). Rated B because the Reye's syndrome figures come from surveillance reports. The fall in cases and the warning happened in sequence; this is not a controlled trial. The benefit is rated "large" because after the warnings, cases fell from 555 a year to 36 or fewer.

### 3. If you are over 60, have had stomach bleeding, or take anticoagulants or steroids, ask a doctor before taking ibuprofen-type painkillers
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=health -->
- Cost: Free. Ask a doctor or pharmacist once.
- Plain language: Ibuprofen, naproxen and aspirin-type painkillers can damage the stomach. In trials using large doses, people taking ibuprofen had about four times the risk of stomach bleeding or perforation as people not taking it. Risk is higher for people who are older, have had stomach bleeding, take anticoagulants or steroids, or drink more than three glasses of alcohol a day.
- Benefit: These drugs are called NSAIDs; common ones are ibuprofen, naproxen and diclofenac. A meta-analysis pooled 280 randomized trials of these drugs against placebo, involving 124,513 people. Compared with placebo, the risk of upper gastrointestinal perforation, obstruction or bleeding was: ibuprofen RR 3.97 (95% CI 2.22–7.10, about four times), naproxen 4.22 (2.71–6.56), and diclofenac 1.89 (1.16–3.09). All of these drugs roughly double the risk of heart failure. US rules require OTC labels for these drugs to carry a stomach-bleeding warning. The warning lists six higher-risk groups: people over 60; people who have had a stomach ulcer or bleeding; people taking anticoagulants or steroids; people taking another drug of this class at the same time; people who drink more than three glasses of alcohol a day while using it; and people who take more than the label says or take it for longer than the label says.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Coxib and traditional NSAID Trialists' (CNT) Collaboration; Bhala N, Emberson J, Merhi A, et al. (2013). Vascular and upper gastrointestinal effects of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: meta-analyses of individual participant data from randomised trials. Lancet, 382(9894):769-779. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(13)60900-9>；21 CFR 201.326(a)(2)(iii)(A) Stomach bleeding warning. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>
- Notes: The paper's conclusions are about high-dose use. It gives no separate figure for occasional use for one or two days at the label dose. Many combination cold medicines also contain this class, so taking them with a painkiller doubles up; check the ingredient list first. If a doctor has told you to take low-dose aspirin long-term to prevent clots, do not stop it on your own, and do not add another painkiller. For using these drugs for period pain and when to see a gynecologist, see section 3, item 25 (taking ibuprofen-type painkillers for period pain).

### 4. For children under 2 with a cold, do not give combination cold medicines or cough medicines on your own
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health who=infant -->
- Cost: Free, and you save the cost of buying the medicine.
- Plain language: China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) requires a warning on 14 children's combination cold medicines. The warning says parents should not give them to children under 2 without guidance from a doctor or pharmacist. Trials in children show that cough medicines and antiallergy drugs work no better than placebo.
- Benefit: The NMPA's 2021 notice No. 57 revised the labels of 14 products, including an-fen ma-mei oral solution, pediatric an-fen huang-na-min granules, pediatric an-fen amantadine granules and others. The new warning reads: "Parents or guardians should not give this product to infants and young children under 2 on their own; use it only under the guidance of a doctor or pharmacist." The precautions also say: "Use strictly according to the label's dose and usage to avoid overdose." They add: "Avoid combining with other cold medicines that contain the same or similar active ingredients." A Cochrane systematic review included 29 randomized controlled trials, 10 of them in children. In child trials, none of these drug types beat placebo: cough suppressants, antihistamines (the antiallergy component), antihistamines plus decongestants (the nasal decongestant component), and cough suppressants plus bronchodilators. Twenty-one studies reported side effects; products with antihistamines and dextromethorphan caused more side effects.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: 国家药监局关于修订氨酚麻美口服溶液等14个品种药品说明书的公告（2021年第57号）及附件. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/directory/web/nmpa/xxgk/ggtg/ypggtg/ypshmshxdgg/20210423105404141.html>；Smith SM, Schroeder K, Fahey T (2014). Over-the-counter (OTC) medications for acute cough in children and adults in community settings. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (11):CD001831. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001831.pub5>
- Notes: China only: Many of these 14 products have "an-fen" in their names and also contain acetaminophen, so giving them with a fever medicine doubles up; see item 1 of this section (acetaminophen). The review found one trial in which honey beat placebo, but honey must not be given to children under 1; see section 20, item 6 (honey). Rated B because each drug type in the review had few trials, and the authors did not pool the figures. The benefit is rated "medium" as a judgment: these drugs have no proven benefit for small children, and the risks are mainly overdose and duplicate ingredients.

### 5. After 20 weeks of pregnancy, do not take ibuprofen-type painkillers on your own
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health who=pregnancy -->
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: After about 20 weeks of pregnancy, ibuprofen-type painkillers may harm the fetus's kidneys and reduce amniotic fluid. US reports of these drugs include cases of newborn deaths. Low-dose aspirin prescribed by a doctor is not covered by this warning.
- Benefit: The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a safety warning in October 2020. Using NSAIDs from about 20 weeks of pregnancy onward can cause kidney problems in the fetus, leading to low amniotic fluid. The warning covers both prescription and OTC drugs, including aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and celecoxib. The FDA reviewed adverse-event reports up to July 2017 and found 35 cases of low amniotic fluid or newborn kidney dysfunction, all with serious outcomes. Five of these newborns died, all with newborn kidney failure. In the published literature, most cases of low amniotic fluid recovered within 72 hours to 6 days after the drug was stopped. The FDA advises pregnant women not to use these drugs from 20 weeks onward unless a doctor specifically recommends it. Low-dose aspirin of 81 mg, prescribed by a doctor for a specific pregnancy problem, is not restricted by this rule. When a painkiller or fever reducer is needed, the FDA says other drugs such as acetaminophen may be used; ask a pharmacist or doctor first.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2020-10-15). FDA recommends avoiding use of NSAIDs in pregnancy at 20 weeks or later because they can result in low amniotic fluid. Drug Safety Communication. <https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic>
- Notes: The FDA warns that many OTC medicines for colds, flu and insomnia also contain these ingredients, so check the ingredient list. US OTC labels used to warn only about the last 3 months of pregnancy; this change moves the cut-off to around 20 weeks. For low-dose aspirin to prevent preeclampsia, see section 27, item 5 (low-dose aspirin). Rated B because the figures come from adverse-event reports, and it is not known how many people took the drug without any problem. The benefit is rated "medium" as a judgment: the outcome is serious, but it happens rarely.

### 6. For heartburn, buy omeprazole yourself for no more than 7 days; for trouble swallowing, vomiting blood or black stools, go straight to hospital
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: Free. Take it for the number of days stated on the label.
- Plain language: Omeprazole enteric-coated tablets have been sold without a prescription since 2022. The label says to take them for no more than 7 days, and not again within two months. Do not self-treat if swallowing is hard or painful, if you vomit blood, or if you pass blood or black stools, since these can signal serious illness. The drug can also mask the symptoms of stomach cancer.
- Benefit: In 2022, China's NMPA notice No. 68 switched omeprazole enteric-coated tablets from prescription to OTC status and published a model label. The model label's indication is "short-term relief of heartburn and acid reflux caused by excess stomach acid." The precautions state: use must not exceed 7 days; if symptoms do not improve, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Do not take it again within two months, and if symptoms recur, seek medical care immediately. Do not use it if you have trouble or pain when swallowing, vomit blood, or pass blood or black stools, as these may be signs of serious conditions. If you have obvious weight loss, repeated vomiting, trouble swallowing, vomiting blood or black stools, malignant tumors must be ruled out first, because treatment may mask symptoms and delay diagnosis. If you are over 55 and have new symptoms or changes in your symptoms, consult a doctor. People taking clopidogrel should avoid taking omeprazole at the same time. The reason is in the US label for clopidogrel (Plavix): omeprazole inhibits the enzyme CYP2C19, which converts clopidogrel in the body. The label says: "omeprazole was shown to reduce significantly the antiplatelet activity of Plavix when given concomitantly or 12 hours apart". Taken together, or 12 hours apart, the two drugs significantly weaken clopidogrel's clot-preventing effect. The label's uses are acute coronary syndrome, recent heart attack, recent stroke and peripheral artery disease.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: 国家药监局关于奥美拉唑肠溶片处方药转换为非处方药的公告（2022年第68号）及附件2《非处方药说明书范本》. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/yaopin/ypggtg/20220826171517123.html>；Sanofi-Aventis U.S. (2025). PLAVIX (clopidogrel) tablets, US prescribing information, sections 1, 5 and 7（DailyMed）. <https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=de8b0b67-eb25-4684-83b5-7ad785314227>
- Notes: China only: Clopidogrel is a clot-preventing drug often used by people who have had a heart attack or stroke. If you take it, ask a doctor before using omeprazole, since omeprazole weakens it. Rated B because these are label requirements, and no study has calculated how much harm is avoided by following them. The benefit is rated "medium" as a judgment: its main value is avoiding delayed diagnosis of stomach bleeding and stomach cancer, but most heartburn is not serious.

### 7. Don't ask a doctor for antibiotics for a common cold
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: It costs nothing, and it saves you the price of the medicine.
- Plain language: Antibiotics do not make a cold go away any faster. For adults, the risk of side effects is more than double the risk of not taking them. For a runny nose with pus lasting less than 10 days, antibiotics also have no clear benefit.
- Benefit: A Cochrane systematic review pooled 6 randomized trials with 1,147 people. For a cold, antibiotics versus placebo made no clear difference in the proportion of people who did not get better or had symptoms that lasted (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.60–1.14, not statistically significant). The risk of adverse reactions was 1.8 times that in the placebo group (95% CI 1.01–3.21). In adults it was 2.62 times (1.32–5.18), and in children there was no increase (0.91, 0.51–1.63). For acute purulent rhinitis, a runny nose with pus lasting under 10 days, antibiotics had no clear benefit either (RR 0.73, 0.47–1.13). Adverse reactions did rise (RR 1.46, 1.10–1.94, about 46% higher). The authors concluded that routine antibiotic use is not recommended for either condition.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Kenealy T, Arroll B (2025). Antibiotics for the common cold and acute purulent rhinitis. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 11:CD000247. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000247.pub4>
- Notes: China only: This version was republished in 2025, and its abstract says the literature search ran up to 2013. In China, antibiotics are prescription-only, and pharmacies sell them only against a prescription, so do not take leftover antibiotics yourself. The benefit is rated "medium" as a judgment call: antibiotics prevent side effects, not death. Also do not ask for an IV drip for a cold; see section 6, item 35 (asking for an IV drip for a cold or fever).

### 8. For diarrhea, drink oral rehydration salts first, and do not give anti-diarrhea drugs to children under 5
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: A box of oral rehydration salts costs from a few to over ten yuan (RMB). Keep one box at home.
- Plain language: Rehydration matters more than stopping diarrhea. Mix oral rehydration salts as the label says, and skip fruit juice or soft drinks, which are too sugary and can make dehydration worse. Do not give children under 5 loperamide or similar drugs, which can cause a bowel blockage that can be fatal.
- Benefit: The WHO manual on diarrhea treatment states that antidiarrheal drugs and antiemetics have no real benefit for acute or persistent diarrhea in children. They do not prevent dehydration or improve nutrition. The Chinese edition of the manual says, in its own words: "Some drugs have dangerous and sometimes fatal side effects. These drugs must never be used in children under 5." Loperamide and similar drugs that slow gut movement can cause severe paralytic bowel obstruction, which can be fatal, and can also prolong infection. The manual also states that sugary drinks, such as soft drinks and commercial fruit juice drinks, pull water from the body into the intestine and cause hypernatremic dehydration. The WHO manual recommends low-osmolarity formulas, with a total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L. Compared with the old formula, it cut unplanned intravenous fluid use by 33%. A Cochrane review pooling 8 trials found similar results (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.45–0.79, about 40% fewer).
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: World Health Organization (2005). The treatment of diarrhoea: a manual for physicians and other senior health workers, 4th revision. <https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/43209>（中文版《腹泻治疗：医生和高年资卫生工作者使用手册》第 2.6 节，同一页面下载）；Hahn S, Kim S, Garner P (2002). Reduced osmolarity oral rehydration solution for treating dehydration caused by acute diarrhoea in children. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (1):CD002847. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002847>
- Notes: When buying, check the osmolarity on the label and choose a low-osmolarity formula. The manual says that when a child's stool contains blood, it is most likely bacillary dysentery, which needs a doctor and antibiotics, not just home rehydration. The benefit is rated "medium" as a judgment call: the figures compare two rehydration salt formulas, not drinking versus not drinking.

### 9. Take no more than 15 days of painkillers a month, or no more than 10 days of combination painkillers, or your headaches may come from the medicine itself
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: It costs nothing. You need to keep a count of the days you take the pills, and resist reaching for them out of habit.
- Plain language: If you get headaches often and take painkillers on many days each month, the pills may be causing them. Doctors call this medication-overuse headache. Most people improve once they stop taking too much medicine.
- Benefit: The 3rd edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, from the International Headache Society, gives the diagnostic criteria. A person with an existing headache disorder who has headaches on 15 or more days a month and regularly takes headache medicine in excess for more than 3 months meets the criteria. For non-opioid painkillers such as paracetamol (acetaminophen), aspirin and ibuprofen, the excess threshold is 15 or more days a month. For combination painkillers, the threshold is 10 or more days a month. A combination painkiller contains two or more painkilling drugs together, or an added ingredient such as caffeine, for example paracetamol with codeine. If you rotate several non-opioid painkillers, add up the days across all of them. The classification notes that epidemiological evidence from several countries shows that more than half of people with headaches on 15 or more days a month have this type of headache. Most patients improve after they stop taking the excess medication, and preventive headache treatment works better.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache Society (2018). The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition. Cephalalgia, 38(1):1-211. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102417738202>；ICHD-3 在线版 8.2 Medication-overuse headache（含 8.2.3、8.2.5）. <https://ichd-3.org/8-headache-attributed-to-a-substance-or-its-withdrawal/8-2-medication-overuse-headache-moh/>
- Notes: Painkillers whose Chinese brand names include the syllable for caffeine (ka) usually contain caffeine, so count them as combination products with the 10-day limit. If the number of headache days keeps rising each month, see a neurologist rather than relying on more medicine. The evidence is rated B because the classification gives diagnostic criteria, not controlled trials. The benefit is rated "medium" as a judgment call: it gives back headache-free days and does not involve death.

### 10. Regularly clear out expired and unused medicines, and put them in the hazardous waste bin with their packaging rather than keeping them at home
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: It costs nothing. Check your medicine cabinet once a year. It takes about ten minutes.
- Plain language: Nearly half of the medicines young children take unsupervised in the US are prescription tablets. Clearing out unused medicine leaves fewer things for children to find. Put it, packaging and all, in the hazardous waste bin, or take it to a pharmacy that collects medicine.
- Benefit: US emergency department surveillance data estimate that from 2004 to 2013, children under 6 made about 640,000 emergency visits (estimate 640,161) after swallowing medicine without supervision. In 2013 alone there were about 59,000 visits (59,092). From 2010 to 2013, the most common form was oral prescription tablets, at 45.9%. Among prescription drugs, the most common were opioid painkillers (13.8%) and benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotics (12.7%). In 2017, five US poison control centers questioned parents who called for help. Of children who swallowed prescription medicine, 30.7% had taken their grandparents' medicine. More than half (51.5%) of the prescription medicines had already been taken out of their original packaging by an adult before the child got hold of them. In China, the National Catalogue of Hazardous Wastes (2025 edition) lists waste medicines and their packaging generated in daily household life as hazardous waste within household waste. Shanghai's household waste sorting guidelines place waste medicine in the hazardous waste category. The guidelines say waste medicine includes expired drugs, capsules, tablets and inner medicine packaging, and that "waste medicine should be disposed of together with its packaging." Shanghai also says district market regulators set up temporary transfer points for expired household medicines collected by pharmacies. The US FDA says the best way to dispose of expired and unused medicines is to take them to a collection site.
- Evidence level: B

### 11. Avoid alcohol while taking cephalosporin or metronidazole antibiotics and for 7 days after stopping them, and avoid alcohol-containing medicines such as Huoxiang Zhengqi Water and cough syrup
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: It costs nothing. Go more than a week without alcohol, and check the ingredients of any other medicine for ethanol before you take it.
- Plain language: Some cephalosporin and metronidazole antibiotics cause flushing, sweating and a racing heart with alcohol, and sometimes breathing trouble or low blood pressure. Alcohol-containing medicines, such as Huoxiang Zhengqi Water (a Chinese herbal remedy) and cough syrup, can do the same. Avoid both during treatment and for 7 days after.
- Benefit: This is called a disulfiram-like reaction. Fujian Provincial Drug Regulatory Bureau's 2023 safe-medication science bulletin says that drinking alcohol before or after taking certain cephalosporin antibiotics can trigger this reaction. The bulletin says it "can seriously harm health and even cause life-threatening danger." Symptoms include facial flushing, sweating, palpitations, and in severe cases shortness of breath, low blood pressure and anaphylactic shock. Drawing on the drugs listed in the clinical medication notes of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, the bulletin names cefazolin, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone, metronidazole, tinidazole and furazolidone, among others. It states that "for 7 days after taking the drug, do not drink alcohol or eat or take foods and medicines containing alcohol," and gives examples such as liquor-filled chocolates and Huoxiang Zhengqi Water. The reason given is that the enzyme blocked by the drug often takes 4 to 5 days to recover. A hospital in Guangzhou compiled 82 cases over 2 years. Cephalosporins caused 57 (69.51%), and nitroimidazoles (metronidazole, tinidazole and similar drugs) caused 10 (12.20%). Alcohol came from alcoholic drinks in 40 cases (48.78%) and from alcohol-containing medicines in 31 (37.80%). Oral products included Huoxiang Zhengqi Water, cough syrup and motherwort fluid extract. Of the 82 cases, 68 were mild, 9 moderate and 5 severe. Symptoms resolved within 1 to 3 hours, and there were no deaths. The US metronidazole prescribing information requires avoiding alcohol during treatment and for at least 3 days after, and avoiding products containing propylene glycol.
- Evidence level: B

- Notes: Not every cephalosporin causes this, but ordinary people cannot tell which ones do, so it is simplest to follow this advice for all of them. When a doctor prescribes a cephalosporin or metronidazole, mention whether you have had any alcohol in the past few days. ### 12. If you take a cold or antihistamine medicine containing chlorphenamine (chlorpheniramine) or diphenhydramine, do not drive, work at heights or operate machinery while taking it
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: It costs nothing. Check the ingredient list when you buy a medicine. On the days you take it, travel by bus or taxi, or ask a doctor or pharmacist to switch you to a non-drowsy medicine.
- Plain language: Many cold medicines contain antihistamines such as chlorphenamine and diphenhydramine, which cause drowsiness. Do not drive, work at heights or operate machinery while taking them. In a driving simulator, diphenhydramine impaired driving more than alcohol did. Not feeling sleepy does not mean you are driving well.
- Benefit: A 2010 bulletin from the National Food and Drug Administration on adverse drug reactions says that Vitamin C Yinqiao Tablets (a Chinese cold remedy) contain chlorphenamine maleate, also called chlorpheniramine. Its adverse effects are mainly drowsiness, weakness, sleepiness and dry mouth. The bulletin advises doctors and pharmacists to tell patients: while taking the medicine, do not drive motor vehicles, boats or other machines, do not work at heights, and do not operate machinery or precision instruments. In 2020, the National Medical Products Administration's public medication advisory said that many combination cold medicines contain antihistamines such as chlorphenamine and diphenhydramine, which cause drowsiness. People who drive, operate machinery or work at heights should use them with caution. A US driving simulator trial recruited 40 drivers aged 25 to 44 with seasonal allergic rhinitis. In different weeks, each driver received a different treatment and drove for 1 hour: diphenhydramine 50 mg, fexofenadine 60 mg, alcohol to a blood alcohol concentration of about 0.1%, or placebo. Fexofenadine is a non-drowsy antihistamine. For keeping pace with the car ahead, diphenhydramine gave the worst results, while alcohol and fexofenadine were both noticeably better. For staying in lane, alcohol and diphenhydramine both made driving worse. When a car ahead suddenly blocked the road, reactions were slowest after alcohol, averaging 2.21 seconds, versus 1.95 seconds after fexofenadine. Fexofenadine and placebo performed about the same. The authors concluded that diphenhydramine affects driving more than alcohol does. Drivers' own reports of drowsiness did not predict how well they drove, so "do I feel sleepy?" should not decide whether you drive.

### 13. If you take a long-term medicine such as a cholesterol-lowering or blood-pressure drug, check the label for grapefruit warnings, and if it mentions grapefruit, avoid grapefruit, pomelo and grapefruit juice
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: It costs nothing. When you get a medicine you will take long-term, check the package insert's drug interactions and precautions sections. If grapefruit is mentioned, do not eat grapefruit, pomelo, bitter orange or lime, or drink their juice while taking the drug. If you love grapefruit, ask your doctor whether you can switch to a medicine that is not affected.
- Plain language: Grapefruit can raise some medicines in your blood several times over, so the dose acts like a larger one. One grapefruit or a glass of juice is enough, and the effect can last more than a day. In serious cases it can damage muscles and kidneys or cause a deadly heart rhythm. Pomelo does the same.
- Benefit: A 2013 review by Canadian pharmacology researchers says that more than 85 drugs are known or expected to interact with grapefruit. Of these, 43 can cause serious adverse reactions. Serious reactions include torsades de pointes, a heart rhythm disorder that can lead to sudden death; rhabdomyolysis (large-scale muscle breakdown that can damage the kidneys); bone marrow suppression; respiratory depression; gastrointestinal bleeding; and kidney injury. Furanocoumarins in grapefruit permanently disable an enzyme in the small intestine (CYP3A4) that breaks down drugs. Less drug is broken down in the gut, so more reaches the blood. One whole grapefruit or 200 mL of juice is enough to raise blood drug levels to a clinically significant degree. For the blood-pressure drug felodipine taken with grapefruit juice, blood levels averaged 3 times those taken with plain water. The same 250 mL of juice produced anywhere from 0 to 8 times the level, depending on the person. For the cholesterol-lowering drug simvastatin taken with 200 mL of juice daily for 3 days, total drug exposure (AUC) was 330% of that with water. Atorvastatin with juice gave 180% to 250%. Pravastatin, rosuvastatin and fluvastatin are not affected. The effect is greatest when juice is taken within 4 hours before the drug. Half remains after 10 hours and a quarter after 24 hours. The review says bitter orange (often used in marmalade), lime and pomelo have the same effect. Sweet oranges such as navel and Valencia do not contain furanocoumarins and do not have this effect. The US FDA's consumer information names simvastatin, atorvastatin and nifedipine. It says that drinking large amounts of grapefruit juice causes too much of the cholesterol drug to stay in the body, increasing the risk of liver and muscle damage and possibly leading to kidney failure.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Bailey DG, Dresser G, Arnold JMO (2013). Grapefruit–medication interactions: forbidden fruit or avoidable consequences? CMAJ, 185(4):309-316. <https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.120951>；U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2021). Grapefruit Juice and Some Drugs Don't Mix. <https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/grapefruit-juice-and-some-drugs-dont-mix>
- Notes: Not all drugs are affected, and the effect varies within the same drug class. The review's list of common drugs includes the cholesterol-lowering drugs simvastatin, lovastatin and atorvastatin, and the blood-pressure drugs felodipine and nifedipine. It also includes amiodarone, ticagrelor, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, domperidone, quetiapine and oxycodone, among others. Clopidogrel works the other way: grapefruit can make it ineffective. If the label does not mention grapefruit and you are unsure, ask your doctor or pharmacist. The review says people aged 45 and over buy the most grapefruit and also take the most medicines. Older people who take felodipine and eat grapefruit may see blood pressure fall without the heart rate rising to compensate. The evidence is rated B because the figures come from pharmacokinetic trials measuring blood drug levels, and serious outcomes come only from case reports. The benefit is rated "small" as a judgment call: the endpoint is blood drug levels, and the research cannot show how many accidents would be avoided.
