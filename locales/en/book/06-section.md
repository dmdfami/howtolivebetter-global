# 6. The avoid list

This section covers things that look cheap and effective. Once you check the original research, their benefit is close to zero, or zero, and some are even negative.

The entries are grouped by theme below. The numbers in brackets are item numbers.

**Supplements**: multivitamins (item 1), plain fish oil (item 2), vitamin D (item 3), antioxidant supplements to prevent cancer (item 4), chondroitin for knee osteoarthritis (item 5), vitamin C against colds (item 6), spending a lot on health supplements (item 10), long-term probiotics (item 11), detox or bowel-cleansing products (item 13), extra calcium after a fracture (item 30), melatonin as a cure for long-term insomnia (item 34).

**Check-ups and minor findings**: whole-body PET-CT and tumor marker packages (item 7), general check-up packages (item 18), not taking medicine for high uric acid if you have never had pain (item 19), not removing gallstones if they have never caused pain (item 20), not avoiding calcium to prevent kidney stones (item 21), not having arthroscopic clean-up for knee pain from wear (item 33).

**Health myths**: relying on a band to lose weight (item 8), buying organic food (item 9), cold showers to boost immunity (item 12), not forcing yourself to drink water (item 14), blue-light glasses (item 16), treating blood donation as a health measure (item 17), eating breakfast or intermittent fasting for weight control (item 26), relying on correct lifting posture to prevent back pain (item 29), demanding an IV drip for a cold or fever (item 35).

**Paying for luck and mood**: paying for fortune-telling or astrology (item 15), buying crystal charms and similar objects (item 22), shopping to improve your mood (item 23), paying more to move up one tier (item 24), not saving because you feel you could die at any time (item 32).

**Willpower and sex**: willpower works like a muscle (item 25), masturbation harms the body (item 27), sexual orientation conversion (item 28).

**AI answers**: relying on an AI chatbot to judge your illness yourself (item 31).

### 1. Don't take multivitamins to live longer or prevent heart disease
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: About RMB 0.5 to 2 a day, or RMB 200 to 700 a year. Taking it takes no time, it is one pill. Taking it for a long time can create dependence, so you may feel you have missed something on days you skip it.
- Plain language: 14,000 male doctors took multivitamins for eleven years. Their rates of serious heart and blood-vessel events, and of death, were no different from those who took none. Pooling nine trials with over 50,000 people showed no effect on death. Not buying them saves RMB 200 to 700 a year. This does not cover people confirmed to be short of a nutrient, or pregnant women taking folic acid.
- Benefit: The Physicians' Health Study II (PHS II) enrolled 14,641 male doctors and followed them for 11.2 years (median; half were followed longer, half shorter). Those who took the pills and those who did not had the same rate of major heart and blood-vessel events. Major cardiovascular events HR 1.01 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means no difference between the two groups). The 95% CI 0.91–1.10 is the plausible range for this number. Deaths were the same too: all-cause mortality HR 0.94 (0.88–1.02). In 2022 the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), which grades preventive measures, pooled 9 randomized controlled trials with 51,550 people and found no link between multivitamins and overall death. Its grade was I, meaning the evidence is insufficient to say. It looks like good value because "one pill covers all your nutrients" sounds sensible, and it costs only a few jiao (tenths of a yuan) a day.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>；US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>；Gaziano JM 等 (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>（备注里那项癌症结果）
- Notes: Disputed: the same male doctors were also followed for cancer. Those who took the pills had about 8% lower cancer incidence (0.86–0.998, P=0.04, just enough to suggest the result is not chance). Cancer deaths were about 12% lower (0.77–1.01), but that range crosses 1, so it could be chance. The benefit is small and has only been seen in men. This does not cover people confirmed to be deficient, pregnant women (who need folic acid), or people whose diet is very poor; follow your doctor's advice for them.

### 2. Don't take plain fish oil to prevent heart disease
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: About RMB 1 to 3 a day, or RMB 400 to 1,000 a year. Taking it takes no time.
- Plain language: Two large trials enrolled 26,000 people and 15,000 people with diabetes. Taking 1 gram of fish oil a day did not change the rates of serious heart and blood-vessel events or of death, compared with not taking it. "Eating fish is good for you" does not mean "fish oil capsules are good for you." Not buying them saves RMB 400 to 1,000 a year.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people, who took 1 gram of fish oil a day and were followed for 5.3 years (median). No difference was seen in heart and blood-vessel events or in deaths. Major cardiovascular events HR 0.92 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means no difference between the two groups). The 95% CI 0.80–1.06 is the plausible range for this number. It crosses 1, so no difference can be detected. All-cause mortality HR 1.02 (0.90–1.15). The ASCEND trial enrolled 15,480 people with diabetes, also taking 1 gram a day, and followed them for an average of 7.4 years. It likewise found no difference: serious vascular events RR 0.97 (0.87–1.08), all-cause mortality RR 0.95 (0.86–1.05). It looks like good value because many people treat "eating fish is good for you" as if it meant "fish oil capsules are good for you."
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>；ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>；Bhatt DL 等 (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>（备注里那项高纯度处方鱼油试验）
- Notes: Disputed: one trial did show a benefit. The REDUCE-IT trial enrolled 8,179 people who already had heart disease or diabetes, were taking statins, and had elevated triglycerides (135–499 mg/dL). They took 4 g a day of prescription pure EPA. The rate of events was 17.2% versus 22.0%, so risk was about a quarter lower (HR 0.75, 0.68–0.83). But that was a high-dose prescription drug for high-risk patients, not supplement fish oil for ordinary people. Eating fish itself is not covered here either.

### 3. Don't take vitamin D to live longer if you are not short of it
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: About RMB 0.2 to 0.5 a day, or RMB 100 to 200 a year. The money is not much. The real cost is believing you have already taken care of prevention.
- Plain language: People who were not short of vitamin D took it for five years, and their rates of cancer, heart disease and death were no different from those who did not. Adults who rarely go outdoors can take 400 IU a day, and no more than 2,000 IU. High doses cause more bone loss and more falls. Tiredness alone does not mean you are short of vitamin D. If you are tired for weeks without a clear reason, see a doctor.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people, who took 2,000 IU of vitamin D a day and were followed for 5.3 years (median). No difference was seen in cancer, heart and blood-vessel disease, or death. For cancers that spread, HR 0.96 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means no difference between the two groups; the 95% CI 0.88–1.06 is the plausible range). Major cardiovascular events HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12). All-cause mortality HR 0.99 (0.87–1.12). The D-Health trial enrolled 21,315 Australians aged over 60. They took 60,000 IU a month and were followed for five years. This trial also found no difference; all-cause mortality HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18), with deaths at 5.3% versus 5.1%. It looks like good value because some studies only recorded data without any intervention, and found that people with low vitamin D levels died more. This was misread as meaning that supplements fix it.
  People who rarely go outdoors have separate recommendations. The UK Department of Health advises that adults who rarely go out, live in care homes, or cover most of their skin when outdoors take 10 micrograms (µg) of vitamin D daily, all year round. The UK National Health Service (NHS) states that 1 µg equals 40 IU, so 10 µg is 400 IU. China's health-industry standard recommends 10 µg a day for adults aged 18 to 64 and 15 µg for those over 65. The same standard sets the tolerable upper intake at 50 µg (2,000 IU) a day, because taking more can push blood calcium too high. The NHS sets a higher upper limit for adults, 100 µg (4,000 IU). Tiredness has many causes; the NHS lists lack of sleep, stress and depression, iron-deficiency anemia, diabetes, an overactive thyroid and sleep apnea. It advises seeing a doctor if you are tired for weeks without a clear cause, or if tiredness affects your daily life. Taking more does not work better.
  VITAL also calculated fractures: people taking 2,000 IU a day had the same rate as those not taking it (HR 0.98, 0.89–1.08). VITAL also looked at depression among 18,353 people over 50 who did not have depression at the start. Those taking vitamin D had 12.9 cases of depression or relapse per 1,000 people per year, versus 13.3 in the group not taking it, a difference of none (HR 0.97, 0.87–1.09). Mood scores also showed no difference. The authors wrote that the results do not support using vitamin D to prevent depression in adults.
  A Canadian trial enrolled 311 healthy people aged 55 to 70 without osteoporosis. They were split into three groups, taking 400, 4,000 or 10,000 IU a day for three years. The higher the dose, the more bone density fell at the wrist's radius: 1.2%, 2.4% and 3.5% in the three groups. No difference was seen in bone strength. The authors concluded that this does not support high-dose vitamin D for bone health. A Swiss trial enrolled 200 people over 70 who had recently fallen. They took 24,000 IU or 60,000 IU a month for one year. The 60,000 IU group did not improve in leg and foot function, and more of them fell during the year (66.9% versus 47.9%). An Australian trial enrolled 2,256 women over 70 and gave them 500,000 IU once a year, in autumn and winter. Their number of falls was about 15% higher (RR 1.15, 1.02–1.30), and fractures were about 26% higher (RR 1.26, 1.00–1.59).
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>；Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>；NHS. Vitamin D. <https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/>；NHS. Tiredness and fatigue. <https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/tiredness-and-fatigue/>；国家卫生健康委员会. WS/T 578.4—2018 中国居民膳食营养素参考摄入量 第4部分：脂溶性维生素，表1. <https://www.nhc.gov.cn/wjw/yingyang/201805/3dcd74635d8e40bcb5d834d3b2f66964/files/1739783549670_23371.pdf>；LeBoff MS et al. (2022). Supplemental Vitamin D and Incident Fractures in Midlife and Older Adults. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2202106>；Burt LA et al. (2019). Effect of High-Dose Vitamin D Supplementation on Volumetric Bone Density and Bone Strength: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2019.11889>；Bischoff-Ferrari HA et al. (2016). Monthly High-Dose Vitamin D Treatment for the Prevention of Functional Decline: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.7148>；Sanders KM et al. (2010). Annual high-dose oral vitamin D and falls and fractures in older women: a randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.594>；Munns CF et al. (2016). Global Consensus Recommendations on Prevention and Management of Nutritional Rickets. J Clin Endocrinol Metab. <https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2015-2175>；Mondal K et al. (2014). A Randomized controlled trial on safety and efficacy of single intramuscular versus staggered oral dose of 600 000IU Vitamin D in treatment of nutritional rickets. J Trop Pediatr. <https://doi.org/10.1093/tropej/fmt105>；Okereke OI et al. (2020). Effect of Long-term Vitamin D3 Supplementation vs Placebo on Risk of Depression or Clinically Relevant Depressive Symptoms and on Change in Mood Scores: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.10224>；Mikola T et al. (2023). The effect of vitamin D supplementation on depressive symptoms in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2022.2096560>；Ghaemi S et al. (2024). The effect of vitamin D supplementation on depression: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Psychol Med. <https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291724001697>
- Notes: Disputed: neither VITAL nor D-Health specifically recruited people who were truly short of vitamin D, so the conclusion applies only to people who were not deficient to begin with. People who are confirmed to be deficient, people with osteoporosis, and infants should still take vitamin D as their doctor advises. Severe deficiency does not necessarily require injections. The 2016 global consensus on nutritional rickets recommends oral dosing, because it corrects levels faster than intramuscular injection. One trial enrolled 61 children with rickets. One group received a single 600,000 IU injection; the other took 60,000 IU orally each week for 10 weeks. After 12 weeks, lab tests and X-rays showed no difference between the two groups. If you finish a prescribed course and the deficiency is still not corrected, have it rechecked and ask your doctor whether absorption is the problem. People who get little sunlight can take 400 IU a day to prevent deficiency. China's health-industry standard states that deficiency causes rickets, osteomalacia and osteoporosis. This does not show that vitamin D prevents cancer or heart disease, or that it treats tiredness. If your doctor has prescribed a different dose, follow your doctor. Whether vitamin D helps people who already have depression is disputed. A 2023 analysis pooling 41 trials found a small drop in depression scores, but results varied widely between studies, and the authors rated the quality of evidence at the lowest level. A 2024 analysis broken down by trial length found no effect in trials lasting more than one year. If you have depression, see a psychiatrist or psychologist first; do not use vitamin D in place of treatment. This entry looked at all-cause mortality, cancer, heart and blood-vessel disease, bones, falls and depression, and did not examine other outcomes. For how to supplement infants, see section 20, item 16 (400 international units of vitamin D a day).

### 4. Don't take antioxidant supplements (beta-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin A) to prevent cancer
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: About RMB 0.5 to 2 a day. For people who smoke, the real cost is a higher risk of lung cancer.
- Plain language: Antioxidant supplements are not just useless. Across 78 trials with nearly 300,000 people, those taking them had a slightly higher death rate in the better-designed trials. In two large trials of smokers, beta-carotene raised the rate of lung cancer by 18% to 28%, and the death rate by 8% to 17%. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos should avoid them in particular.
- Benefit: The Cochrane review pooled 78 randomized controlled trials with 296,707 people. For people taking antioxidant supplements, all-cause mortality RR 1.02 (RR is the risk ratio; 1 means no difference between the two groups; the 95% CI 0.98–1.05 is the plausible range). Looking only at the better-designed trials, which are less prone to bias, RR 1.04 (1.01–1.07), slightly higher. Beta-carotene alone RR 1.05 (1.01–1.09); vitamin E alone RR 1.03 (1.00–1.05). The ATBC trial enrolled 29,133 male smokers, who took 20 mg of beta-carotene a day. Their rate of lung cancer was 18% higher (3%–36%), and their all-cause mortality was 8% higher (1%–16%). The CARET trial enrolled 18,314 smokers or people exposed to asbestos. Their risk of lung cancer was about 28% higher (RR 1.28, 1.04–1.57), and their risk of death about 17% higher (all-cause mortality RR 1.17, 1.03–1.33). It looks like good value because the reasoning chain "antioxidants fight aging, and fighting aging prevents cancer" sounds like common sense at every step.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>；The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>；Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), the body that grades preventive measures, gave beta-carotene and vitamin E a grade D in 2022, meaning it recommends against using them. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos have already been harmed by these supplements. Antioxidants naturally present in vegetables and fruit are not included; keep eating those.

### 5. Don't count on glucosamine or chondroitin for knee osteoarthritis
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: About RMB 2 to 5 a day, or RMB 700 to 1,800 a year.
- Plain language: In a trial, people with knee osteoarthritis who took a placebo for 24 weeks, about 60% also said the pain had eased. Glucosamine or chondroitin added only about four to five percentage points more, which is small enough to be chance. For RMB 700 to 1,800 a year, you get roughly the same effect as the placebo.
- Benefit: The GAIT trial enrolled 1,583 people with knee osteoarthritis and followed them for 24 weeks. Among those taking the placebo (a sugar pill), 60.1% also reported pain relief. Glucosamine was 3.9 percentage points higher (P=0.30). Chondroitin was 5.3 points higher (P=0.17). Taking both together was 6.5 points higher (P=0.09). These gaps are all too small to rule out chance. In the same trial, the active control drug celecoxib was 10.0 points higher (P=0.008). It looks like good value because "raw material for cartilage" sounds exactly right for the problem, and taking a placebo alone already leads six in ten people to say it works.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: Disputed: in the same trial, among people whose pain was severe at the start, 79.2% of those taking both responded, versus 54.3% of controls (P=0.002). But the authors say this was found after the fact in the data, so it can only be treated as a lead, not a conclusion. The approach with real evidence for knee osteoarthritis is weight loss and muscle strengthening; see item 33 of this section (arthroscopic clean-up).

### 6. Don't rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=low scope=money -->
- Cost: Fizzy tablets cost about RMB 1 to 2 a tablet, or RMB 300 to 700 a year.
- Plain language: Taking vitamin C every day will hardly reduce how often you catch colds, and starting it once you have a cold works inconsistently. Daily use shortens how long a cold lasts by about 8%. Whether that is worth several hundred RMB a year is up to you to work out. Marathon runners and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception: among them, those who took it caught about half as many colds.
- Benefit: The Cochrane review pooled 29 controlled trials with 11,306 people. Ordinary people who took vitamin C every day hardly reduced how often they got colds. Cold incidence RR 0.97 (RR is the risk ratio; 1 means no difference between the two groups; the 95% CI 0.94–1.00 is the plausible range). Trials that started vitamin C after a cold began report "no consistent effect": some showed a benefit, some did not. In the same review, regular daily use shortened how long cold symptoms lasted by about 8% in adults (95% CI 3%–12%) and by 14% in children (7%–21%). Marathon runners, skiers and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception: in 5 trials with 598 people, they had about half as many colds (incidence RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.35–0.64). It looks like good value because it is cheap and tastes sweet and sour, and the idea that "vitamin C prevents colds" has circulated for half a century.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: Disputed: in the same review, daily use did shorten how long cold symptoms last: about 8% in adults (3%–12%) and 14% in children (7%–21%). By this calculation, a cold is about half a day shorter. Marathon runners, skiers and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception: in 5 trials with 598 people, they had about half as many colds (RR 0.48, 0.35–0.64). Most people belong to neither group.

### 7. Don't get a whole-body PET-CT or a tumor marker package when you have no symptoms
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: A whole-body PET-CT costs about RMB 7,000 to 10,000 per scan, and exposes you to ionizing radiation. Tumor marker packages cost several hundred RMB. The real cost comes afterward: an "abnormal" finding that turns out to be a false alarm (called a false positive) leads to repeat tests, tissue biopsies and surgery, and the money and suffering are all in that stage.
- Plain language: People with no symptoms who get checked mostly find false alarms. Over 80% of people who have a whole-body CT are found to have at least one "abnormal" result, and nearly 40% are called back for further tests, though most of these are benign. For ovarian cancer screening in women with tumor markers, deaths from ovarian cancer did not fall, and some women had surgery for a false alarm that was unnecessary.
- Benefit: The USPSTF, the US body that grades preventive measures, gave a grade D to screening asymptomatic women for ovarian cancer with CA-125 and ultrasound, meaning it recommends against it. The basis is the PLCO trial. Ovarian cancer deaths were 0.34% in the screened group and 0.29% in the usual-care group, RR 1.18 (95% CI 0.82–1.71). Screening did no good. Across trials, 0.2% to 3.25% of people underwent unnecessary surgery because of a misdiagnosis (false positive), and up to 15% of these people had serious surgical complications. Another study gave whole-body CT screening to 1,192 people with no symptoms. Of these, 86% had at least one "abnormal" finding, and 37% were advised to have further tests. The authors wrote that "most findings were described as benign." It looks like good value because "check the whole body in one go, catch it early" fits intuition so well.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA（同一份建议另有 USPSTF 官方页）. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>、<https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>；Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: The tumor marker part is backed by randomized controlled trials. The whole-body CT and PET-CT part relies only on follow-up records of what happened to people who had those scans, with no control group, so the evidence is one grade weaker, equivalent to grade B. The screenings with solid evidence, such as for colorectal, cervical and breast cancer, and low-dose chest CT for high-risk people, are a different matter; see section 1. For people who already have symptoms, or who have been diagnosed with cancer, PET-CT is diagnosis rather than screening, and is a separate question.

### 8. Don't expect activity-tracking bands or watches to help you lose weight
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: The device costs RMB 200 to 2,000. You also spend time each day looking at the data.
- Plain language: Overweight people on the same diet and exercise plan lost different amounts depending on the device. Those wearing an activity band lost 3.5 kg over two years, while those not wearing one lost 5.9 kg. To lose weight, a device is not a must-buy.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial enrolled 471 overweight or obese adults and followed them for 24 months. Everyone followed the same diet and exercise plan; the only difference was whether they wore a wearable device. The wearing group lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5, the plausible range). The non-wearing group lost 5.9 kg (5.0–6.8), more than the wearing group. The difference between the groups was 2.4 kg (1.0–3.7, P=0.002). It looks like good value because "you can only manage what you measure" sounds sensible, and you only need to buy the device once.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: This rests on a single trial, using arm-worn devices from the early 2010s, so it does not show that all wearable devices are useless. Exercise itself works; see section 2. The conclusion comes down to one line: to lose weight, a device is not a must-buy.

### 9. Don't pay a premium for organic food for "better health"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: Usually 1 to 3 times the price of comparable conventional food.
- Plain language: There is no strong evidence that organic food is more nutritious. Only 3 studies have looked at how people's health fared after eating it, and they found no difference. Organic produce is 30 percentage points less likely to test positive for pesticide residues, but testing positive does not mean the food exceeds the limit.
- Benefit: A systematic review combined 17 studies in people and 223 studies measuring food composition. Its conclusion: "there is a lack of strong evidence that organic foods are significantly more nutritious than conventional foods." Only 3 of these studies looked at how people's health fared after eating the food (they looked at allergies and symptomatic infections), and they found no difference between the two groups. Organic produce is 30 percentage points less likely to test positive for pesticide residues, but testing positive does not mean the food exceeds the limit. It looks like good value because "no pesticides means safer, and safer means healthier" sounds like common sense at every step.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: Studies that directly look at health outcomes in people who eat organic food are very rare. So the conclusion is "there is no evidence it is better," not "it has been shown to do no good." Environmental impact, animal welfare and taste are not covered here. Eating more vegetables and fruit is good in itself, whether or not they are organic.

### 10. Don't spend big money on health supplements, paste preparations or tonics to "regulate" your body
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=money -->
- Cost: From several hundred RMB to tens of thousands of RMB. Gift packaging often multiplies the price several times.
- Plain language: Multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D and other common ingredients were each checked in items 1 to 6 of this section, and none showed a benefit. The line on the package saying health supplements are not medicines and cannot replace medicine is required by the rules that govern them. If you spend this money on your parents and bring it as a gift, the sentiment is fine. The product choice is the problem.
- Benefit: What you get back is the money you keep. The reasons are in items 1 to 6 of this section. The most common ingredients in health supplements (multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine and vitamin C) showed no expected benefit in randomized controlled trials. Their figures are in the Benefit fields of those six entries. Regulatory rules only say what a product may claim; they are not evidence that the product does nothing. China's State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) requires that the front of a health-supplement package carry, across at least 20% of its area, the line: "Health supplements are not medicines and cannot replace medicine to treat disease." The same rules limit what health supplements may be used for to "supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving the body's health status, or reducing the risk factors for disease." It looks like good value because "it doesn't treat disease, it just regulates" cannot be shown to be false. And money spent on gifts runs on social obligation, not on evidence.
- Evidence level: C

### 12. Don't force yourself into cold showers to "boost immunity"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=low scope=money -->
- Cost: Costs no money. Each shower means 30 to 90 seconds of discomfort, and in winter you need willpower to keep going. People with cardiovascular disease should be careful, because cold exposure carries risk for them.
- Plain language: In a trial of 3,018 people, those who took cold showers took 29% fewer sick days off work. But their total days off sick were the same as everyone else's, so you can't say they got sick less often. In 2025, pooling 11 trials showed that inflammation markers rose briefly after a cold shower, while immune markers did not change. Take cold showers if you enjoy them, but don't treat them as a health investment.
- Benefit: A randomized controlled trial in the Netherlands enrolled 3,018 people. After a hot shower, they added 30, 60 or 90 seconds of cold water. These people took 29% fewer sick days off work (IRR 0.71, a ratio of frequencies). But the paper states that "there were no significant differences between groups in days of sick leave." Total days off sick were the same on both sides. No meaningful differences appeared in quality of life, work efficiency or anxiety. A 2025 systematic review pooled 11 randomized trials with 3,177 people. Right after cold immersion and one hour later, inflammation markers actually rose. Immune markers did not change. Stress fell only at the 12-hour time point. The authors say the evidence is "limited by the small number of RCTs and small sample sizes," meaning there are too few trials of this kind and too few people in each. It looks like a good deal because it costs nothing, and the idea that "suffering makes it work" is too appealing.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>；Cain T et al. (2025). - Notes: Disputed: the number of sick-leave episodes did drop by 29%, and that gap does not look like chance. But total sick days did not drop, so you can't say people actually got sick less. Athletes using cold water to recover after training is a different question, which this entry does not cover. No clear harm has been seen for healthy people from cold showers, but don't treat them as a health investment.

### 15. Don't pay for fortune-telling, tarot or horoscopes to make decisions
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: Costs no money. The hard part is giving up a shortcut for making decisions, because from now on you have to think things through yourself.
- Plain language: In a classic classroom experiment, students each got a "personality analysis" that was actually identical for everyone, yet most found it very accurate. That is why fortune-telling seems accurate. Astrologers pick the right match about as often as random guessing. The real cost is more than a few dozen yuan. A fortune-telling ring in Xi County, Henan, charged "incense donations" and "support packages" of up to 3,888 yuan (RMB), and its ringleader was sentenced to eleven years in prison.
- Benefit: In a classic classroom experiment, students who took a personality test received an "individual analysis" that was actually the same for everyone, yet most considered it accurate. The author concluded that "being accepted by the subject or the analyst does not prove the explanation is correct." Thirty-nine students rated the analysis from 0 to 5 for accuracy, and 34 gave it a 4 or 5. Based on the distribution in the table, the average was about 4.26. This explains why fortune-telling always seems accurate; the phenomenon is called the Barnum effect. Astrology was also tested in a double-blind experiment published in Nature. Astrologers had to pick, from three personality test results, the one that matched a birth chart. They picked 40 out of 116. Random guessing would pick about 38.5, and the astrologers themselves predicted they would pick 58.5. Official case: the Supreme People's Procuratorate publicized a fraud ring in Xi County, Henan, that ran online fortune-telling. It first lured people in with free palm readings, then charged "incense donations" and "support packages" of up to 3,888 yuan (RMB). More than 50 people were victims, over 2 million yuan (RMB) was recovered, and the ringleader was sentenced to eleven years in prison and fined 100,000 yuan (RMB).

- Notes: Both of these are single studies. The Forer paper itself does not give an average score; 4.26 is calculated from the score distribution in its Table 1. This only says that these methods do not predict accurately; it does not judge any belief. Paying a few dozen yuan for entertainment is not counted here. ### 16. Don't buy blue-light glasses to "protect your eyes," don't believe "months of screen use will ruin your eyes," but treat painful, red eyes as an emergency

- Cost: Costs no money, and saves the several hundred to over a thousand yuan (RMB) premium charged for the lenses.
- Plain language: Blue-light filtering lenses show no clear benefit, so you can save the several hundred to over a thousand yuan (RMB) premium by not buying them. Compared with ordinary lenses, people wearing them have no difference in eye strain or in how sharply they see, and no evidence shows they protect the back of the eye. Eye strain and dryness from looking at screens recover with rest; they do not permanently damage your eyes. What really damages eyes is welding arc light, lasers, and an acute glaucoma attack.
- Benefit: A 2023 Cochrane systematic review included 17 randomized controlled trials, each with 5 to 156 people, and follow-up ranging from under one day to 5 weeks. Critical flicker fusion frequency, an objective measure of eye fatigue, showed the two groups "may have almost no difference," with low certainty. ### 17. Don't treat blood donation as health care: claims that it "detoxes, lowers blood lipids or prevents heart attacks" have no randomized trial support, and frequent donation really does cause iron deficiency
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: Costs no money. One donation, including filling in forms, drawing blood and resting afterwards, takes about an hour. For the following weeks, your body keeps replacing the iron you lost.
- Plain language: The claims that donating blood detoxes you, lowers blood lipids or prevents heart attacks do not hold up. People who keep donating were already healthier to begin with. Once that effect is removed, the drop in death rate from donating one extra time a year shrinks from nearly 20% to 7.5%. In trials that compared people head to head, deaths did not differ. On the other hand, donating too often can really leave you weak and cold, because of iron deficiency.
- Benefit: The Finnish Kuopio study followed 2,862 men aged 42 to 60 for an average of nearly 9 years. Of the 153 who had donated blood in the 24 months before the study started, 1 (0.7%) had an acute heart attack. Of the 2,529 who had not donated, 316 (12.5%) did (P<0.0001 for the difference between the two proportions). After adjusting for age and other coronary risk factors, men who had donated had about 88% lower heart attack risk: relative risk 0.12 (95% CI 0.02–0.86, P=0.035). But this came from recorded follow-up with no control group. The Nordic SCANDAT database includes 1,182,495 blood donors, with 9,526,627 person-years of cumulative follow-up and 15,401 deaths. Adjusting only for characteristics such as age and sex, each extra annual donation was linked to an 18.6% lower mortality rate (95% CI 16.8%–20.4%). Adding a further adjustment for the fact that only people who stay healthy are allowed to keep donating shrinks the reduction to 7.5% (5.7%–9.4%). The authors' conclusion was that this "cannot be interpreted as definitive evidence of health benefit." The FeAST randomized controlled trial enrolled 1,277 people with symptomatic lower-limb arterial disease, randomly assigned to two groups: one had regular blood removal every 6 months to reduce iron, and the other was a control. The main outcome was death from any cause: 125/636 (20%) versus 148/641 (23%). HR 0.85 (HR is the hazard ratio, where 1 means the two sides are the same). 95% CI 0.67–1.08, P=0.17. Counting deaths together with non-fatal heart attacks and strokes, the figures were 180 (28%) versus 205 (32%), HR 0.88 (0.72–1.07, P=0.20). Neither showed a difference. The INTERVAL randomized trial enrolled 45,263 people who gave whole blood, and assigned them to three donation intervals. For men these were 12, 10 and 8 weeks; for women, 16, 14 and 12 weeks. Shorter intervals clearly yielded more blood within two years (men on the 8-week interval gave 1.69 more units than those on 12 weeks, 95% CI 1.59–1.80). Quality of life, physical activity and cognitive function showed no difference between groups. But donation caused more discomfort, including fatigue, shortness of breath, dizziness, lightheadedness and restless legs (leg discomfort at night that makes it hard to lie still). Average hemoglobin and ferritin were lower, and more donations were deferred because hemoglobin was low (P<0.0001 for each). Iron deficiency affects heat production. Ten women with iron-deficiency anemia, eight people whose iron stores were used up but who were not yet anemic, and twelve control women were immersed in 28 °C water for 100 minutes. Rectal temperature was 36.0±0.2 °C in the anemic group versus 36.2±0.1 °C in controls (P=0.001). Oxygen consumption was 5.28±0.26 versus 5.99±0.29 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ (P=0.04). Thyroid hormones thyroxine and triiodothyronine were also lower (P<0.002). This claim looks true because blood donors are healthy people who have been screened repeatedly, and the hypothesis that "draining iron protects the heart" has not been confirmed in randomized trials.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Salonen JT, Tuomainen TP, Salonen R, Lakka TA, Nyyssönen K (1998). Donation of blood is associated with reduced risk of myocardial infarction. The Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study. American Journal of Epidemiology, 148(5), 445-451. <https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a009669>；Ullum H, Rostgaard K, Kamper-Jørgensen M, et al. (2015). Efficiency and safety of varying the frequency of whole blood donation (INTERVAL): a randomised trial of 45 000 donors. - Notes: China only: What is rejected is "donating blood for your own health," not blood donation itself. Blood goes to other people, which under this book's beneficiary tiers falls into tier 4 (strangers). What comes back to you is the benefit the system provides. Article 14 of the Blood Donation Law states that voluntary donors who use blood at a hospital themselves do not pay the fees for collection, storage, separation and testing. Spouses and direct relatives may be exempted or charged less, as the provincial government prescribes. The National Health Commission's January 2024 notice says 28 provinces have already waived these fees at discharge for voluntary donors and their relatives using blood at hospitals within the province. <!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=money -->

- Cost: Costs no money, and saves the long-term cost of medication and regular follow-up tests, as well as the risk of taking a drug you don't need.

### 20. Don't remove your gallbladder preventively just because a check-up found gallstones you have never had pain from

- Cost: Costs no money, and saves a surgery under general anaesthesia and a hospital stay. The obligation you take on is knowing which signs mean you must go to the hospital right away.
- Plain language: Of people who have gallstones but have never had pain, about 80% show no symptoms over nearly nine years. If you leave the stones alone, about 1 to 3 in every 1,000 people each year develop an acute problem such as gallbladder inflammation or pancreatitis. Removing the gallbladder is not risk-free either: about 1.5% of people have their bile duct injured. That is why guidelines in Europe and the UK both say to wait if there are no symptoms.
- Benefit: The Italian MICOL population study ultrasounded 11,229 people aged 29 to 69. It found gallstones in 856, of whom 580 had no symptoms; average follow-up was 8.7 years. Among those 580, 453 (78.1%) stayed symptom-free, 61 (10.5%) developed mild symptoms, and 66 (11.4%) developed severe symptoms. During follow-up, 189 gallbladder removals were performed in total, and 41.3% of them were done on people with no symptoms. The authors concluded that "expectant management remains an effective approach for most patients," where expectant management means watching and waiting without surgery. A US HMO followed 691 people diagnosed with gallstones, with a mean follow-up of 78±61.6 months. Among the 135 without symptoms, followed for 58±50.2 months (median 46.3 months), only 10% developed gallstone symptoms and 7% had surgery. Of the 50 deaths in the whole group, only 2 were related to the biliary tract, and both were in the group with symptoms. The authors concluded that "patients with silent stones do not need surgery before symptoms appear." Silent stones are stones that cause no noise and no attacks. The UK NICE guideline CG188, section 1.2.1, says: "For people with normal gallbladder and biliary tract who incidentally find asymptomatic gallstones, inform them that treatment is not needed until symptoms appear." The 2016 EASL gallstone guideline puts both sides of the risk together. People with no symptoms develop symptoms at about 0.7% to 2.5% a year. The yearly rate of acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, obstructive jaundice (the bile duct is blocked and the person turns yellow) or cholangitis is 0.1% to 0.3%. Complication rates only rise to 1% to 3% a year after the first colic attack, which is why most people feel pain once before something serious happens. On the surgical side, in Sweden's national registry of 51,041 gallbladder removals, 1.5% injured the bile duct, and about one fifth of those (0.3%) were partial or complete bile duct transections. People with bile duct injury had a 3.9% one-year mortality, versus 1.1% for those without. The guideline also notes that 10% to 40% of patients still have discomfort after surgery, or feel better for a while and then have symptoms return. The guideline concludes that surgery for people without symptoms, whether open or laparoscopic, does not prolong life, because the risks of surgical death and complications outweigh the risk of not operating. Treating once symptoms appear also costs less overall. A 1983 analysis gave specific numbers: for a 30-year-old man, preventive removal, compared with waiting, meant an average of 4 fewer days of life; at 50, it was 18 days. No randomized trial has ever compared surgery with no surgery. It looks like a good deal because the idea that "it will hurt sooner or later, so remove it while young" seems sensible.

### 21. Don't cut calcium out of your diet to prevent kidney stones
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=health -->
- Cost: Costs no money. In fact it is simpler, since you don't have to constantly watch which foods contain calcium.
- Plain language: People who have had calcium oxalate stones are often told to eat less calcium, but a trial found the opposite. Men with recurrent stones who ate a normal amount of calcium while cutting back on animal protein and salt had half the recurrence rate over five years compared with those on a low-calcium diet. The UK guideline also states that calcium should not be restricted.
- Benefit: A 5-year randomized controlled trial enrolled 120 men with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and high urinary calcium (idiopathic hypercalciuria). They were randomly assigned to two groups. One group ate a normal amount of calcium, 30 mmol a day. This group also cut back on animal protein (52 g a day) and salt (50 mmol sodium chloride a day). The other group followed a traditional low-calcium diet of 10 mmol a day. Over 5 years, recurrence was 12/60 versus 23/60. The unadjusted relative risk was 0.49 (95% CI 0.24–0.98, P=0.04), meaning the normal-calcium group had half the risk of recurrence. During follow-up, urinary calcium fell by about 170 mg a day in both groups. But urinary oxalate differed: it rose by an average of 5.4 mg a day in the low-calcium group and fell by an average of 7.2 mg a day in the normal-calcium group. Section 1.8.1 of UK NICE guideline NG118 states that daily calcium intake should not be restricted. Adults should keep to 700 to 1,200 mg a day; children and teenagers, 350 to 1,000 mg a day depending on age. Cutting calcium looks like a good deal because the idea that "stones are made of calcium, so eat less calcium" seems obvious.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Borghi L, Schianchi T, Meschi T, et al. (2002). Comparison of two diets for the prevention of recurrent stones in idiopathic hypercalciuria. New England Journal of Medicine, 346(2), 77-84. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa010369>；National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2019). Renal and ureteric stones: assessment and management. NICE guideline NG118，第 1.8.1 条. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations>
- Notes: Disputed: this trial was done only in men, all of whom had recurrent calcium oxalate stones with high urinary calcium, so it cannot be applied directly to all kinds of stones. The trial group also changed three things at once: normal calcium, less animal protein and less salt. So you cannot credit all the benefit to "not cutting calcium." Low salt and less animal protein may also have contributed. What this rejects is actively cutting out calcium-containing foods, not the idea that you should get more calcium. - Cost: Costs no money. What you need to do is not count "bringing luck" or "nourishing" as reasons to buy it. The hard part is staying out of it when everyone around you is wearing one and talking about how well it works.

### 23. Don't expect buying things to improve your mood or your sense of status
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: Costs no money. The hard part is holding back from ordering when you are in a bad mood.
- Plain language: People who value money and possessions more highly report being less happy, but the link is weak, and it is unclear which causes which. Other research found that money spent on an experience makes people happier than money spent on things. So for the same amount of money, a trip out is more likely to make you happy than a full shopping cart.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis (combining many studies) covered 259 independent samples and 753 effect sizes. It found that people who value material things more report lower well-being. On the most widely used multidimensional scale, the correlation was r = −0.19 (corrected ρ = −0.24). On scales that measure only the money dimension, the association was weaker, at r = −0.08 to −0.11. Strength also varies by the outcome measured. The strongest links were with risky health and consumer behaviour and negative self-evaluation, at r = −0.28 to −0.44. The weakest were with life satisfaction and negative emotion, at r = −0.13 to −0.15. The authors also ran a mediation analysis (checking what passes the link along), which suggested the negative association may arise because psychological needs go unmet. Another study: in two surveys of different populations, respondents said money spent on an experience made them happier than money spent on things. In a later lab experiment, recalling the good mood after spending money on an experience was also higher than recalling buying something. (This does not apply to China's national population; both used samples from other countries.)
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409>；Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Notes: Disputed: most of the studies included asked a group of people about two things at the same point in time, so they cannot tell which came first. The authors themselves say that clarifying the direction requires more experiments and long-term follow-up. So this cannot be read as "buying things makes you unhappy." It can only be read as "counting on buying things to make you happy is unlikely to work." The association is also weak: r = −0.19 explains only a few percentage points of the difference, and people vary a lot. Both studies used foreign samples, and there is no comparable data for Chinese populations to cite. This does not oppose spending on things you love. It opposes using shopping as a way to manage your mood, because then your first reaction when you are in a bad mood is to place an order. For concrete ways to save money, see section 5. For what has real evidence for getting out of a low mood, see the second half of section 22 and section 29. For a budget increase to "stand a notch above others," see item 24 of this section.

### 24. Don't spend extra on a home, car or social circle just to move up a notch among the people around you
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=money -->
- Cost: No money. Before paying a premium, ask one more question: does this extra money buy the thing itself, or my position among this group? The hard part is not following when everyone around you has upgraded.
- Plain language: Among people with similar incomes, the richer their neighbors, the less happy they report being. This is strongest with neighbors who have the same education, especially ones you socialize with. Spending more to live where everyone is better off can leave you a rung lower. But if everyone's income rises together, people still feel better off.
- Benefit: The National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) in the US re-interviewed the same people twice. The author matched this data to average local income (PUMA), which is estimated from the 5% sample of the 1990 census. After subtracting the person's own income and a set of personal characteristics, the result was: the higher the neighbors' income, the lower the self-reported well-being. The effect is large, holds under a different method, and is highly significant. The author's wording is that a rise in neighbors' income and an equal-sized drop in one's own income reduce well-being by the same order of magnitude. When each person is compared with their own earlier answers (individual fixed effects), the direction and similar size hold, but significance drops to the 10% level. When comparisons are limited to the same state and the same survey wave (state and wave fixed effects), the result barely changes. This indicates the pattern is not explained by people who like to move to poorer places being happier to begin with, or by unmeasured state-level factors. The effect is stronger among people who often socialize with their neighbors, and not significant among those who only socialize with friends outside the community. Renters and homeowners showed no difference, which does not support the idea that high house prices in expensive areas leave less money to spend. Well-being falls only with the income of neighbors who have the same education, and barely responds to neighbors with different education. The effect works mainly through satisfaction with one's material situation, not through satisfaction with family life or other areas. Finally, if your income and your neighbors' incomes rise by the same proportion, well-being still rises.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Luttmer EFP (2005). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(3), 963–1002. <https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/120.3.963>；Luttmer EFP (2004). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. NBER Working Paper No. 10667（本条引用的表述与数量级按这一版逐字核对）. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667>
- Notes: Reasons for grading B: the data come from a questionnaire that re-interviewed the same people, there is only this one study, and the sample is from abroad. The outcome is the respondent's own reported well-being, not money. The author used statistical methods to rule out several confounders, such as comparing the same person over time or within the same state and wave, but this is still not a randomized trial. There is no comparable Chinese data to cite. The size of the benefit is graded "medium", following the convention used in item 23 of this section (don't expect buying things to improve your mood). The money saved may well reach the 10,000-yuan range (RMB), but there is no figure for how much well-being that money would buy, so it is not graded "large". This does not oppose spending on a bigger home, a quieter neighborhood or a shorter commute; those buy the thing itself. ### 25. Don't believe that willpower is like a muscle that runs out after use
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: No money. Only one way of thinking needs to change.
- Plain language: The popular idea is that using willpower on one task makes the next one harder. Across 23 labs and 2,141 people repeating the same test, the effect was almost zero. Stop explaining yourself with "I've run out of willpower today," and don't pay for courses that teach you to build it up.
- Benefit: A multi-lab, preregistered replication involved 23 labs and 2,141 participants. They followed the standardized protocol of Sripada et al. for the sequential task paradigm: first a task that uses self-control, then a second task that requires self-control. The pooled result shows a very small ego-depletion effect, with a 95% confidence interval that includes 0 (d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15). The authors used this to discuss whether the model of self-control as a limited resource still holds. Another replication with a similarly preregistered design, 36 labs and 3,531 people, found d = 0.06, which is also too small to matter.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>；Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Notes: What this refutes is the model in which self-control is a resource that gets used up, not the fact that people get tired. Lack of sleep and working too long reduce efficiency; that is a separate matter, covered by the sleep and working-hours items in section 3. What randomized trials support is changing your environment and how you frame tasks: see item 10 of section 4 (move things you don't want to touch farther away) and item 1 of section 4 (write it as "what time, where, and what to do when X happens"). Fixing what you wear and eat is still worth doing because it costs almost nothing. It saves you a few minutes and a few rounds of indecision, not willpower, so don't expect it to make your judgment better in the afternoon. ### 26. Don't expect skipping breakfast or 16:8 intermittent fasting to control your weight; choose meal times you can keep up long term
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: No money. What you save is the daily mental effort of fighting your meal schedule.
- Plain language: The popular claim is that skipping breakfast makes you overeat later. Studies don't show that: breakfast-skippers ate about 260 kcal less a day and were slightly lighter. Over a year, 16:8 did no better than just eating less. Long-term breakfast skippers have higher heart risk, but that is only an association.
- Benefit: First, the claim that skipping breakfast leads to eating more later. A meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials. Those assigned to eat breakfast consumed 259.79 kcal more per day than those who skipped it (95% CI 78.87–440.71; this is the credible interval). They weighed 0.44 kg more (0.07–0.82). The skippers did not make up the difference at lunch and dinner. These trials were short: weight was followed for an average of 7 weeks and intake for an average of 2 weeks. Next, 16:8 and similar patterns that restrict eating to a few fixed hours. In one study, 116 overweight adults followed it for 12 weeks. Those who ate only between noon and 8 p.m. and those who ate three regular meals a day differed in weight by −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78), not significant. Another study, conducted in Guangzhou, followed 139 patients with obesity for 12 months. Time-restricted eating plus calorie control lost 1.8 kg more than calorie control alone (−4.0 to 0.4), also not significant. Finally, whether long-term breakfast skipping is harmful. Across a pooled 2,380,000 people, breakfast skippers had about 17% higher cardiovascular disease risk (OR 1.17, 1.09–1.26) and about 49% higher cardiovascular death risk (OR 1.49, 1.20–1.84). But these studies only recorded habits and did not assign anyone to groups. In an original study that followed people for 17 to 23 years, the all-cause mortality risk ratio was 1.19, with a range of 0.99 to 1.42. That range crosses 1, so it is not a clear difference.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Sievert K, Hussain SM, Page MJ, et al. (2019). Effect of breakfast on weight and energy intake: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. ### 29. Don't expect learning "correct lifting posture" or taking manual-handling training to prevent back pain; reduce the weight and frequency of lifting, and exercise regularly

- Plain language: Workers who took lifting-technique training had no less back pain than those who did not. How far people bend while lifting did not clearly predict back pain. - Benefit: First, training. The 2011 Cochrane systematic review pooled 9 randomized trials of 20,101 workers. Compared with people who watched a single video, the RR was 0.93 (0.69–1.25), also similar. The evidence quality is moderate. The authors concluded that lifting advice and training, with or without lifting aids, do not prevent back pain or the disability it causes. Next, bending. A 2020 systematic review pooled studies on how much people bend the lumbar spine when lifting; the evidence quality is low. In the only cohort study, people who later developed back pain and those who did not differed by only 1.5 degrees in maximum bend (−0.7 to 3.7), not significant. Nine of the 11 studies found no difference between groups. Seven other studies used a different measure, and in those, people with back pain bent less during lifting, by 6.0 degrees (−11.2 to −0.9). The authors concluded there is no evidence that bending more causes back pain. Last, what to do. A meta-analysis of 8 cohort studies found that each 10 kg of load per lift raised back pain risk, OR 1.11 (1.05–1.18), about 11% higher.
