# 3. Don't waste your energy

This section only counts the ledger of energy and time. It looks at four things: the attention you can use each day, how fast you react, how often you make mistakes, and how long it takes to get back on track after an interruption. Most of the numbers are measured directly, either in experiments or by following groups of people and recording what happened (cohort studies). These numbers are not converted into lifespan or mortality rates. Most entries in this section have evidence level B. That is simply how this field works; it does not mean the advice is not valid. Item 19 covers what to expect when dealing with police, doctors and bank tellers. It cites regulations, not experiments, so its level is C. Item 15 (treating pessimistic thoughts as a symptom) and item 22 (treating "others demand perfection from me" as a symptom) cite numbers on mortality and suicide risk. Their basis differs from the other entries in this section, so they are not compared with them.

The entries in this section are grouped by theme below. Item numbers are in brackets.

**Attention at work**: turn off non-essential notifications (item 1), process messages once a day in batches (item 5), block out interruptions of even a few seconds (item 6), do one thing at a time (item 7), use earplugs in open-plan offices (item 12), and do not keep adding weekly work hours (item 13).

**Sleep and staying up late**: fix your wake-up time (item 2), sleep 7 to 8 hours a night (item 3), no caffeine after 2 p.m. (item 4), no screens for an hour before bed (item 8), go to bed on time (item 9), no major decisions late at night (item 10), take a 10-minute nap if you feel drowsy in the afternoon (item 11), try cognitive behavioural therapy first for long-term insomnia (item 26), and if you snore with breathing pauses or feel sleepy during the day, get checked for sleep apnoea (item 27).

**Thoughts that won't leave you**: when you keep replaying something, switch to a hands-on task (item 14), treat pessimistic thoughts as a symptom (item 15), when you feel embarrassed, halve your estimate of how bad it was (item 21), and treat "others demand perfection from me" as a symptom (item 22).

**Anger and low mood**: when angry, leave the situation first (item 17), what to do first when your mood is low (item 18), do not vent anger on a punching bag (item 23), and write down what is on your mind over several days (item 24).

**Relationships and social media**: learn to refuse requests you do not want to accept (item 16), treat police and doctors as people doing a rule-bound job (item 19), and set limits on apps that show you your peers' posts (item 20).

**Pain**: for period pain, take a painkiller such as ibuprofen (item 25).

### 1. Turn off non-essential notifications, and keep your phone out of sight while working
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. Changing the settings takes a few minutes and barely needs willpower.
- Plain language: Even if your phone buzzes and you don't look at it or touch it, your score on attention tasks drops. The drop is about the same as if you had actually taken a call or sent a text. Another effect: if your phone sits on the desk and you resist looking at it, the mental room you have left still shrinks. People who depend on their phones most lose the most.
- Benefit: Merely receiving a notification, without looking at or touching the phone, makes performance on attention tasks noticeably worse. The drop is comparable to actually making a call or sending a text. This was done in a lab, with university students as subjects. Two other experiments found that even when people successfully resist looking, having their own phone nearby reduces the mental capacity they have available. People with strong phone dependence drop the most.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>；Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Notes: These were all single tasks in a lab. No one has measured what happens when this adds up day after day. For jobs that require being reachable at all times, you can keep notifications for just a few contacts.

### 2. Fix your wake-up time, weekends included
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. You have to give up sleeping in on weekends, which takes a bit of willpower.
- Plain language: The fifth of people with the most irregular schedules has a body clock about 2.5 hours later than the fifth with the most regular schedules. That amounts to living in permanent jet lag. Students with more regular sleep schedules also do better academically.
- Benefit: 61 university students were recorded for 30 days. The fifth with the most irregular sleep-wake patterns had a circadian rhythm (body clock) about 2.5 hours later than the fifth with the most regular patterns. Specifically, melatonin onset was at 00:08 versus 21:32. The time they felt most sleepy and most inclined to sleep was also about 1.8 hours later. The more regular the schedule, the better the academic performance (correlation r = 0.37).
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Notes: This is the only study, it is small, and it had no control group. The researchers used modelling and estimated that the difference in body clocks mainly comes from how much light people are exposed to each day. They suggest light could be used to adjust the clock, but they did not test this experimentally. So, beyond fixing your wake-up time, getting outside in daylight during the day is an inference from their model. For how to catch up after an occasional late night, see section 2, item 38 (catch up on sleep the following night): the answer is to go to bed earlier that night, not to sleep until noon the next day.

### 3. Sleep 7 to 8 hours a night, and don't treat 6 hours as enough
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. You need to set aside 1 to 2 more hours a day for sleep. Going to bed earlier takes some willpower.
- Plain language: After two weeks of sleeping only 6 hours a night, your brain performs as badly as if you had gone two days and two nights without sleep. The problem is that you cannot feel it. Self-rated sleepiness stops rising after the first few days, and people sleeping 6 hours rate themselves just as sleepy as people sleeping 4 hours. So the feeling that "6 hours is enough for me" is not reliable.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept only 6 hours a night for 14 consecutive days. Their cognitive performance declined cumulatively, reaching the level of two consecutive nights of total sleep deprivation. Their self-rated sleepiness rose only in the first few days and then barely increased further. The self-rated sleepiness of the 6-hour and 4-hour groups did not differ. This was a randomised controlled trial (RCT), in which people were randomly assigned to groups. Separately, short-term total sleep loss (acute sleep deprivation, up to 48 hours) has an effect size of g = -0.776 on simple attention lapses and g = -0.125 on reasoning accuracy. This part pools 70 studies.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>；Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Notes: The most important point is that "I don't feel sleepy" is not evidence that you have slept enough. People who have long slept 6 hours have only adapted in how they feel; their performance has not caught up. The part that pools many studies refers to staying up all night. Evidence for chronic short sleep rests on only one randomised controlled trial. The link between sleep duration and risk of death is a separate question, see section 2, item 13 (about 7 hours a night): that entry is based on mortality, and the 7-hour group had the lowest risk.

### 4. No caffeine after 2 p.m.
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. People who are used to afternoon coffee need to resist it, which takes some willpower.
- Plain language: Taking 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed, roughly three to four cups of coffee, cut sleep by more than an hour when measured with instruments that night. Sleep diaries written by the people themselves showed no difference at all. A 250 mL cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to avoid affecting sleep. For someone who goes to bed at 11 p.m., 2 p.m. is the cutoff.
- Benefit: Taking a tablet with 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed reduced total sleep time, measured with instruments, by 1.1 to 1.2 hours compared with a placebo tablet. Counting 107 mg per 250 mL cup of coffee, 400 mg is about three to four cups. The placebo tablets looked identical but contained no caffeine. However, participants' own sleep diaries did not report any difference; they did not notice it themselves. Pooling several studies (meta-analysis): caffeine reduces total sleep time by 45 minutes on average, and sleep efficiency (the proportion of time in bed actually spent asleep) falls by 7%. A 250 mL cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to avoid affecting sleep.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>；Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Notes: The Drake study was run by only one lab and used 400 mg, a high dose equal to a full day's upper limit taken at once; for the limit itself, see section 2, item 26 (three to four cups of coffee). People who metabolise caffeine slowly need an even earlier cutoff. Cutting caffeine gains you sleep time, which adds to the effect of item 3 (sleep 7 to 8 hours a night).

### 5. Process email and messages in a fixed few batches each day
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. You need to take a little time to tell colleagues how often you reply. Resisting the urge to check email constantly takes some willpower.
- Plain language: The same group of people was compared over two weeks. In the week when email checking was limited to three times a day, daily stress was clearly lower, even though they sent and received the same number of emails. Without any limit, people checked their email 15.5 times a day on average.
- Benefit: 124 adults, compared with themselves over two weeks (within-subject comparison). In the week when email was checked three times a day, daily stress was clearly lower than in the week with no limit (effect size Cohen's d = 0.37). The number of emails received and replied to did not differ between the two weeks. At the start, with no limit, people checked email 15.5 times a day on average.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Notes: There is only one study, the effect is small to moderate, and it measured stress, not output. Instant messaging has not been studied directly; applying this to it is an extrapolation.

### 6. When doing work that needs sustained thought, keep out even interruptions lasting just a few seconds
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. Close the door, put on headphones, or hang up a "do not disturb" sign.
- Plain language: An average interruption of 2.8 seconds doubles the chance of error in the next operation. A 4.4-second interruption triples it. Field tracking also found that it takes an average of 25 minutes to get back to an interrupted task, with other tasks squeezed in between. Interrupted people work faster, but their stress, frustration and effort are all higher.
- Benefit: An average interruption of 2.8 seconds doubles the error rate on tasks done step by step in sequence (serial tasks). A 4.4-second interruption triples it. This was done in a lab. Another study was a field observation of 24 office workers (knowledge workers). On average they switched work topics every 11 minutes, and 57% of work topics were interrupted. When they returned to the interrupted task the same day, it took an average of 25 minutes and 26 seconds, with 2.26 other tasks squeezed in between. A further experiment with 48 people found that interrupted people worked faster, but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>；Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>；Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Notes: That field study split interruptions into two kinds: those where someone comes to find you, and those you open yourself. Closing a door only blocks the first kind. For the second kind, combine this with item 1 (turn off non-essential notifications) and item 5 (batch your messages).

### 7. Do one thing at a time, and don't reply to messages during meetings
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. Resisting the urge to reply to messages in meetings takes some willpower.
- Plain language: Once you switch tasks, you react more slowly and make more mistakes. People who often use several media at once have not learned to do several things at once. On task-switching tests, they actually do worse.
- Benefit: After switching tasks, reactions are clearly slower and more error-prone. This comes from a review of several studies. Another lab study used university students as subjects. People who often use several media at once (heavy media multitaskers) are more easily pulled away by irrelevant things and irrelevant memories. On task-switching tests, they perform worse.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>；Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Notes: The extra cost of switching depends heavily on the task, so no single figure is given here. The Ophir study compared people at one point in time, so it cannot tell whether "multitasking weakens attention" or "people with weaker attention are more inclined to multitask."

### 8. No glowing screens for the hour before bed; if you read, use paper or an e-ink screen with no front light
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. Putting your phone down an hour before bed means resisting a long-standing habit, which takes willpower.
- Plain language: Reading on a glowing screen before bed delays falling asleep by about 10 minutes compared with reading a paper book. Melatonin is suppressed by 55%. The next day your body clock is pushed back by more than 1.5 hours, so you feel groggier in the morning and take longer to become fully alert.
- Benefit: In a lab, the same people did both conditions in turn (crossover trial). In one, they read an e-book on an iPad at maximum brightness before bed, for about 4 hours, for 5 nights in a row. In the other, they read a paper book in an equally dim room. Reading a lit e-book before bed delayed falling asleep by about 10 minutes (25.7 minutes versus 15.8 minutes). Evening melatonin was suppressed by 55%. The next-day circadian rhythm (body clock) was delayed by more than 1.5 hours, with melatonin onset at 22:31 versus 21:01. The next morning, people were sleepier and needed longer to become fully alert.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Notes: The study had few participants and was run in a lab with controlled conditions. At everyday phone brightness and usage time, the effect may be smaller. The study did not test the night mode on phones, nor e-ink. An e-ink screen without a front light is read by reflecting room light, like paper, so it is grouped with paper books; this is inferred from this study. An e-ink screen with its front light on also emits light, and there is no data on it.

### 9. Go to bed on time; don't stay up late for games, short videos or porn
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is resisting the "just one more episode" habit over the long term. Keeping your phone out of the bedroom is the easiest approach.
- Plain language: In 2025, Chinese adults slept an average of 6.97 hours a night and fell asleep at 0:10 on average. Among children and teenagers, those who use phones or tablets before bed are more than twice as likely to get too little sleep, and 2.7 times as likely to feel sleepy during the day. Simply having the device in the bedroom, unused, gives similar numbers. So the simplest fix is to keep your phone out of the bedroom.
- Benefit: In 2025, Chinese residents slept an average of 6.97 hours a night and fell asleep at 0:10 on average (China Sleep Research Society). People who use portable devices before bed were about 2.17 times as likely to get too little sleep (OR 2.17), about 1.46 times as likely to have poor sleep quality (OR 1.46), and about 2.72 times as likely to feel sleepy during the day (OR 2.72). Simply having the device in the bedroom without using it gave similar figures: about 1.79, 1.53 and 2.27 times (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27). This part pools 20 cross-sectional surveys covering 125,198 children and adolescents. Another cross-sectional survey of 844 adults aged 18 to 94 found that people who kept using their phones after the lights went out took longer to fall asleep, were less likely to actually sleep once in bed, and were more fatigued during the day.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>；Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>；中国睡眠研究会 (2026). 2026 中国睡眠健康研究白皮书（福建省卫健委转载健康报报道）. <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>；Grubbs JB 等 (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x>（备注里的争议方之一）；Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58>（备注里的争议方之二）
- Notes: Disputed. The cost of staying up late itself is covered in item 3 (sleep 7 to 8 hours a night). On whether porn content affects everyday energy and mental ability, there is no reliable evidence yet that can say whether it is the cause. One analysis pooling several studies argues that so-called "porn problems" stem more from a mismatch between a person's values and their own behaviour than from the content itself. Another study pooled data from 280 men and found that time spent watching had no relationship with erectile function during sex with a partner. This entry only addresses the point that such content eats into sleep time. The figures 6.97 hours and 0:10 do not appear in a press release on the China Sleep Research Society's official website (checked September 2026), so here they follow the Health Daily report reprinted by the Fujian Provincial Health Commission.

### 10. Don't make major decisions or send important messages late at night
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=low scope=time -->
- Cost: No money. You just push these things until after you wake up.
- Plain language: When people are well rested, they quickly learn to avoid high-risk options. After two nights without sleep, they keep choosing the risky ones, and the effect is clearer in older people. How much a few lost hours matter has not been measured directly, so put important decisions off until you have slept.
- Benefit: 34 healthy adults were given the Iowa Gambling Task, a test of risky decision-making, after 49.5 hours without sleep. When they were rested, they learned quickly to avoid high-risk options. After sleep loss, they chose high-risk options more often. Older adults were affected more. This is one experiment, and the only study on this.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Notes: The study looked at the extreme case of two nights without sleep. How an ordinary person does at 2 a.m. has not been measured directly. The "don't send messages" half is the author's experience (level C); sending them the next day is still fine.

### 11. If you feel sleepy in the afternoon, nap for 10 minutes, not 30
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: No money. It takes 10 minutes and a place where you can lie down or rest your head.
- Plain language: After a night of too little sleep, a 10-minute nap gives an immediate lift, and part of the effect lasts about two and a half hours. A 5-minute nap does almost nothing. A 20-minute nap takes 35 minutes to work. After a 30-minute nap, you feel groggy at first, and you perform worse for a while than if you had not slept.
- Benefit: After a night of too little sleep, a 10-minute nap immediately improves sleepiness, fatigue, energy and mental performance, and part of the benefit lasts until 155 minutes later. A 5-minute nap has almost no benefit. The benefit of a 20-minute nap appears only 35 minutes later. After a 30-minute nap, sleep inertia comes first: right after waking, performance is worse, and it improves only later.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Notes: There is only one lab study, with healthy young participants. If you cannot sleep, do not force yourself. No research supports lying with your eyes closed for 10 minutes. Naps longer than an hour have been linked with higher mortality and coronary heart disease risk; see section 2, item 37 (keep naps within half an hour).

### 12. Use earplugs or find a quiet room for memory-heavy work in an open-plan office
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no gain=low scope=time -->
- Cost: Earplugs cost a few dozen RMB. Booking a quiet meeting room takes some effort.
- Plain language: When background noise rises from 39 to 51 decibels, people remember fewer words, feel more tired, and want to work less. During breaks, watching a nature video with sound restores energy better than continuing to listen to office noise.
- Benefit: Students worked in a simulated open-plan office. When background noise rose from 39 to 51 dB (LAeq), they remembered fewer words, rated themselves as more tired, and were less willing to work. During breaks, watching a nature video with sound brought their energy ratings back up better than listening only to river sounds or office noise.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Notes: There is only one lab study. The most disruptive noise is speech you can understand. White-noise headphones may help with this, but the study did not test headphones.

### 13. Don't keep adding weekly hours: past about 49 hours, each extra hour produces less and less
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: You may earn less overtime pay. You may also need to push back with your manager.
- Plain language: Records from British munitions factories in World War I show that under 49 hours a week, output rises with hours worked. Past 49 hours, each extra hour adds less and less output. Working 70 hours produces about the same as working 56 hours, so the extra 14 hours are wasted.
- Benefit: Output records of British munitions factory workers in World War I: under 49 hours a week, output rises in proportion to hours. Past 49 hours, output starts to grow more slowly. It peaks at about 63 hours. Output for 70 hours is almost no different from output for 56 hours, so the extra 14 hours are wasted.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Notes: The data come from factories a hundred years ago, with physical work and mostly female workers. The threshold may not apply the same way to mental work, and the author says other jobs may be higher or lower. What is reliable is the direction: past a certain point, more work adds almost nothing. The specific figure of 49 is not reliable.

### 14. When you catch yourself replaying the same upsetting event, switch to a hands-on task
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is noticing that you are replaying it (this is called rumination), and that noticing takes practice.
- Plain language: Replaying the same upsetting event deepens depression, locks in negative thoughts, makes problems harder to solve, and makes real action harder. It also drains the people who are willing to listen to you. When you notice you are going around in circles, do something hands-on.
- Benefit: A review of many studies says that replaying the same upsetting event (rumination) deepens depression. It also strengthens negative thinking and weakens problem-solving. It gets in the way of action, and it drains the people willing to listen. It is linked with anxiety, binge eating, heavy drinking and self-harm.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Notes: The review gives only the direction, not figures. "Switch to a task" is the author's own practice. The literature supports only one sentence: diverting your attention is better than going in circles. No one has compared which tasks work best.

### 15. Treat thoughts like "things will definitely get worse" as symptoms, not facts
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=health -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is treating such thoughts as symptoms rather than as facts.
- Plain language: In a twin study followed for 20 years, each step up in pessimism was linked with about 13% higher risk of death in the same period, and about 20% higher risk of death from heart disease. High optimism scores did not bring a matching benefit. So the thing to address is pessimism, not "not being positive enough."
- Benefit: 2,978 Australian twins aged 50 and over were followed for an average of 20 years, during which 1,068 died. Each one-standard-deviation rise in the pessimism scale was associated with about 13% higher risk of death in the same period (hazard ratio 1.134). The 95% CI for this figure is 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵. Cardiovascular death was about 20% higher (1.196, 1.045–1.368, P=0.0093). It was unrelated to cancer death. Optimism scores were not clearly linked with mortality either.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Notes: Disputed. This is observation only, with no grouping. Some would say people become pessimistic because they are already ill. The author argues that if so, cancer deaths should also be linked. Unmeasured factors may still play a role. Optimism had no protective effect, so you do not need to force yourself to "think positively." What to do is avoid treating pessimistic expectations as accurate predictions of the future.

### 16. Cut back on relationships that drain you, and learn to refuse requests you do not want to take on
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: No money. In the short term you may face social friction and lose some relationships. Saying no takes real willpower.
- Plain language: A survey of widowed women aged 60 to 89 found that draining interactions were more strongly and more consistently linked with well-being than pleasant interactions were. So cut back first, then add.
- Benefit: In a survey of 120 widowed women aged 60 to 89, draining interactions were more consistently and more strongly linked with psychological well-being than pleasant interactions were. By this result, the harm from a person who drains you is greater than the benefit from a person who supports you.
- Evidence level: C
- Sources: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Notes: The group surveyed is specific, and it was a one-time questionnaire, so the order of cause and effect cannot be seen. It measures well-being, not energy. Placing it in this section is the author's inference. There is no objective standard for which relationships count as draining; you have to judge for yourself.

### 17. When angry, leave the scene first, and treat the other person like weather, not an enemy
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: No money. In the moment you must hold back from retorting and walk away, then decide later whether to deal with it. This takes some practice.
- Plain language: Looking at what happened from a different angle, or putting yourself in the other person's shoes, can calm your emotions. The second works better. Simply not showing your feelings also helps a little. But forcing your feelings down or forcing yourself not to think about it does not work. People who punish the offender end up thinking about it for longer afterward. People who do not retaliate move on faster.
- Benefit: Pooling 306 experimental comparisons (a meta-analysis): reappraising what happened from another angle (cognitive reappraisal) had an effect size on emotion of d = 0.36. Seeing it from the other person's side gave d = 0.45. Shifting attention gave d = 0.27. Suppressing outward expression (not showing it) worked, d = 0.32. Forcing feelings down did not work (d = -0.04), and forcing yourself not to think about it also did not work (d = -0.12). These figures combine measures of feelings, outward expression and bodily reactions; the summary does not report them separately. Three other experiments showed that people who punished the offender later thought about the offender for longer, while people who did not punish moved on faster. ### 20. Don't treat "how others are doing" as daily required reading: limit the apps that feed you peers' updates, or turn them off
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. Setting a limit on an app, or shutting an account down, takes a few minutes. The hard part is not opening it afterward, which means long-term resistance to habit.
- Plain language: People who often compare themselves online with those doing better tend to feel worse and have lower self-esteem. Quitting Facebook for four weeks made people slightly happier but less informed about current events. Capping three social apps at 10 minutes a day each lowered loneliness and depression after three weeks.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis combined several studies: 54 independent samples, 94 effect sizes, N = 36,583, using a three-level random-effects model. The average correlation between online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment was r = 0.330 (95% CI 0.289–0.370). Broken down by outcome: fear of negative social evaluation 0.438, anxiety 0.382, depression 0.306, low happiness 0.268, low self-esteem 0.263. Age, cultural background and the year of data collection did not change the strength of this link. Longitudinal studies did not show stronger results than one-time surveys.
  A randomized controlled trial of 2,743 US Facebook users found that 61% were willing to stop using Facebook for less than 102 US dollars. These people were randomly assigned to a four-week stop group or a control group. For a typical participant, stopping freed up 60 minutes a day. The subjective well-being index rose by 0.09 standard deviations (SD). Subscales: life satisfaction 0.12, anxiety 0.10, depression 0.09, happiness 0.08. These results still held after adjusting for chance findings from multiple comparisons. The authors compared this with psychological interventions that make people more positive. Those raise subjective well-being by about +0.34 SD and lower depression by about −0.23 SD. So this effect is about 25% to 40% of a typical psychological intervention. The price over the same period: the current affairs knowledge index fell 0.19 SD, and political hostility fell 0.16 SD. After the experiment ended, the stop group still used Facebook 12 minutes a day less than the control group (0.31 SD, a 23% drop).
  A second randomized controlled trial included 143 undergraduates, randomly split into two groups. One group limited Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes a day each; the other kept using them as usual. After three weeks, loneliness and depression were clearly lower in the limit group. Both groups had lower anxiety and fear of missing out than at the start.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. - Notes: The "correlation" half only shows that two things occur together. It does not show that one causes the other, and the authors themselves say more long-term studies are needed. What to limit is the kind of use aimed at checking how others are doing. Keeping in touch with family and friends, looking up information and job hunting are not included. If you can't resist, start by turning off notifications, "people you may know" suggestions and home-page recommendations. ### 21. When you feel "everyone has noticed how I screwed up", divide that estimate by two
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=low scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. It only takes asking yourself, when you're tense, whether they really noticed.
- Plain language: People who wore an embarrassing T-shirt guessed nearly half the room would recognize the face, but fewer than a quarter did. After group talks and chats, people overrate how much their slips stand out and underrate how much others like them. When you feel that everyone saw you embarrass yourself, the real number is often half or less.
- Benefit: Study 1: Participants wore a T-shirt printed with a portrait of Barry Manilow and walked into a room with other participants. Wearers estimated that on average 46% of observers could correctly name the person on the shirt. The observers' actual accuracy was 23%, so the estimate was exactly double. Subtracting the actual figure from the estimate for each session, the average overestimate was 23 percentage points (95% CI 9–38). After dropping the two sessions with only two and three observers, it was 27 percentage points (11–43).
  Study 2: Participants wore a T-shirt of their own choice that they thought looked positive. They estimated 48% on average, while observers' actual accuracy was 8%, a sixfold gap. In a footnote, the authors note that the wearers estimated almost identically in both studies (48% and 46%), so the difference lay in observers' accuracy (8% and 23%). They suspect the main reason is that the questionnaire given to observers in Study 2 was more demanding, not that decent clothes are more easily overestimated.
  Study 3: Undergraduates were split into groups of 3 to 7 people. Each group discussed an assigned topic for 20 minutes, then wrote a policy recommendation together. Right after the discussion, each person privately estimated how the others would rank them, where a smaller number means a higher rank. On "how often you said something wrong", people's self-estimated rank averaged 2.30, while others' actual ranks averaged 2.96, a gap of −0.66 (p < .01). So people think the wrong things they said stand out more to others. The same pattern held for "possibly offensive remarks" (2.35 vs 2.90, p < .01). For "remarks others might criticize", the gap was smaller (2.61 vs 2.82, p < .05).
  A separate series of studies covered three settings: strangers paired for conversations, university freshman roommates, and adult workshops. People systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them, and how much those partners enjoyed the talk. The authors call this the "liking gap". It appeared in conversations of different lengths, and it persisted among freshman roommates for months.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211>；Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Notes: Rated B because these are small lab and campus studies. The settings were wearing clothes, chatting with strangers or roommates, and student group discussions, with questions asked right after the discussion ended. There is no direct evidence for extending this to "colleagues gossiping about me" or "will anyone still remember what I said in a meeting days later". The numbers only show the direction and rough size of overestimation; they cannot be used as coefficients. Other people's opinions don't always matter less. In settings where you are really being evaluated, such as job interviews, thesis defenses, performance reviews or medical check-up reports, the evaluation is real, so prepare where needed. What you should discount is the everyday feeling that "everyone is watching me". That part is your own estimation bias, and it's what you have to deal with.

### 22. Treat "others demand perfection from me" as a symptom, not a fact
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=low scope=health -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating this thought as something to work on, rather than as an accurate measure of what people really expect of you.
- Plain language: People who feel that those around them demand perfection, and that falling short means rejection, are more likely to have thoughts of suicide. Self-imposed perfectionism is linked to these thoughts much less, roughly a third as strongly. Even after accounting for earlier thoughts, the first kind still tracks with later increases in suicidal thoughts, though only modestly.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis combined 45 studies with N = 11,747 people, including undergraduates, medical students, community adults and psychiatric patients, using a random-effects model. The figures below are weighted average correlations with suicidal thoughts. Perfectionistic concerns: r = .28 (95% CI .24–.32; 42 studies, 7,936 people). Perfectionistic concerns is a broad grouping that covers socially prescribed perfectionism, worry about making mistakes, doubts about one's actions, and gaps between reality and standards. Socially prescribed perfectionism on its own also gave r = .28 (.25–.32; 30 studies, 3,640 people). Perfectionistic striving: r = .10 (.07–.13; 31 studies, 4,588 people). It combines self-oriented perfectionism with personal standards. Parental criticism: r = .20 (.11–.29).
  Looking at suicide attempts: perfectionistic concerns r = .12 (.07–.17; 15 studies, 5,275 people), and socially prescribed perfectionism r = .19 (.08–.29; 8 studies, 689 people).
  Now the part that was followed over time. After subtracting suicidal thoughts present at the start, socially prescribed perfectionism still moved in the same direction as suicidal thoughts at later follow-up, though only slightly. Self-oriented and other-oriented perfectionism showed no link.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522–542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Notes: China only: Rated B because most of the included studies were one-time questionnaires, so the order of events can't be seen. The correlation is moderate (r around .28), explaining only a few to about 10% of the variation. The outcomes were suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts, not death itself, so the benefit is scored conservatively as a surrogate endpoint. The authors themselves say the suicide-attempt result for perfectionistic concerns may show publication bias, meaning studies with unflattering results were not published. Adding back estimated missing studies does not change the direction of the conclusion. High standards are not a problem in themselves, and you don't have to lower them. The difference lies in where the standards come from, and whether you conclude "I am a failure" when you fall short. Like the pessimistic expectations in item 15 (treating "things will definitely get worse" thoughts as symptoms), this is something you can treat as a symptom. If you have suicidal thoughts, call 12356 first (China's national mental health hotline) and move means of harm out of reach; see section 1, item 25. For how to respond when someone close to you shows this, see section 8, item 15. For depression screening in children, see section 30, item 8.

### 23. When angry, don't vent by punching a bag or running laps; first slow your body down
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. In the heat of anger, switch to a few minutes of something that slows your heart rate: breathe slowly, sit down, be still. The hard part is that the thing you most want to do in the moment is vent.
- Plain language: Venting methods that stir the body up, such as punching a bag, cycling or running, don't calm anger overall. Methods that quiet the body, such as slow breathing, mindfulness and meditation, clearly reduce anger and the urge to lash out.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis combined 154 studies, 184 samples and 10,189 people. Methods that calm the body, such as deep breathing, mindfulness and meditation, reduced anger and aggression: effect size g = -0.63, 95% CI -0.82 to -0.43. The result held across genders, ethnic groups, ages and cultures. Methods that arouse the body, such as punching a bag, jogging or cycling, were overall ineffective: g = -0.02, 95% CI -0.13 to 0.09. The authors concluded that venting anger and going for a run are both ineffective ways to calm down.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ (2024). A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or decrease arousal: What fuels or douses rage? Clinical Psychology Review. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414>
- Notes: The outcome measured is anger and aggression, not energy. Recording the unit as time is the authors' classification: one fewer argument means one fewer mess to clean up. Practical methods you can use on the spot are in section 22, item 8 (cyclic sighing), and item 17 of this section (leave the room when angry). Running is not useless. Over the long term it helps low mood; see section 22, item 6 (when in a bad mood, walk or run). It is just not suited as an on-the-spot way to cool down. Don't drive, send messages or make decisions while angry. If someone is hitting you, leave first and call the police. At that point the problem is not your emotions; see section 8, item 43 (domestic violence).

### 24. If something is weighing on you, writing down your thoughts and feelings for several days in a row may help, but don't expect it to cure anything

- Plain language: Writing down what's bothering you has a small average benefit. The combined results are inconsistent: two analyses found no clear effect. It costs almost nothing and is worth trying. If emotional problems are serious, don't use it instead of seeing a doctor.

### 25. For period pain, take an ibuprofen-type painkiller rather than just enduring it; if pain interferes with work or school, or persists despite three months of medication, see a gynecologist

### 26. For long-term insomnia (more than three nights a week, lasting over three months), try cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia first, rather than taking sleeping pills long term
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=high gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Available at a sleep clinic, psychiatry or psychology department, which charge registration and treatment fees. Online self-help courses are much cheaper. A course usually lasts 6 to 8 weeks, and you keep a sleep diary every day. You can start on your own with stimulus control: get into bed only when sleepy, and get up if you can't sleep lying down. Use the bed only for sleep and sex, get up at the same time every day, and avoid napping during the day. The hard part is sleep restriction: in the first few weeks you cut time in bed, so you feel more tired during the day.
- Plain language: After this treatment, people fell asleep about 19 minutes faster and stayed awake about 26 minutes less at night. The effect fades slowly but lasts 12 months. Online self-help works about as well as in-person treatment. Sleeping pills help people over 60 only a little: 1 in 6 takers gets side effects, and only 1 in 13 sleeps better.
- Benefit: The National Health Commission's 2020 diagnostic guidelines for mental disorders set out the criteria for insomnia disorder under the international classification: (1) difficulty falling asleep, or waking often or too early; (2) daytime fatigue and sleepiness that affect functioning; (3) these occur at least three times a week for at least three months. Under three months is called short-term insomnia disorder. The guideline makes CBT-I the first-line treatment. It has five parts: sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive therapy, relaxation training and sleep hygiene, usually over 6 to 8 weeks. The guideline says its short-term effect is comparable to medication, and better in the long term. It also says sleeping pills taken continuously should generally not exceed four weeks; if they continue, reassess monthly. The American College of Physicians' 2016 guideline strongly recommends that adults with chronic insomnia first receive this treatment. If it alone doesn't work, discuss with a doctor whether short-term medication is appropriate.
  One analysis pooled 20 randomized controlled trials with 1,162 people (a meta-analysis). After treatment, time to fall asleep was reduced by 19.03 minutes (95% CI 14.12–23.93). Wake time after sleep onset was 26.00 minutes less (15.48–36.52). Sleep efficiency, the share of time in bed actually spent asleep, rose by 9.91% (8.09%–11.73%). Total sleep time increased by only 7.61 minutes, not statistically significant (−0.51 to 15.74). No study reported adverse outcomes.
  Another analysis pooled 30 randomized trials to see how long the effect lasts. Compared with untreated people, the improvement in insomnia severity shrank over time: g = 0.64 at 3 months, 0.40 at 6 months and 0.25 at 12 months. The effect weakens gradually, but the authors consider it clinically meaningful within a year.
  For online delivery, an analysis pooled 11 randomized trials with 1,460 people. Every sleep measure improved, with effect sizes g from 0.21 to 1.09. The authors say this is comparable to in-person delivery, and longer courses with more follow-up gave larger effects. Another online randomized trial of 1,711 people compared digital CBT-I with a control group that received only sleep hygiene materials. People on digital CBT-I had large improvements in sleep-related quality of life, and small improvements in daytime physical and mental health.
  Now sleeping pills. An analysis pooled 24 randomized trials with 2,417 people over 60 who had insomnia. Each trial lasted at least five nights. Compared with placebo, drug users slept 25.2 minutes more in total and woke 0.63 fewer times a night. For every 13 people taking the drug, one sleeps better. For every 6, one has side effects (NNH 6, 4.7 to 7.1). Memory and thinking side effects were 4.78 times as common as with placebo (OR 4.78, 1.47–15.47), and daytime fatigue was 3.82 times as common (OR 3.82, 1.88–7.80).

### 27. Loud snoring, breath-holding observed during sleep, or daytime sleepiness so strong you nod off while driving: get a sleep study at a hospital to check for sleep apnea, and for moderate to severe cases, use a CPAP breathing machine at night

- Cost: Start at a hospital sleep clinic, respiratory department or ENT department. Confirmation requires one night of sleep monitoring, either in hospital or by borrowing the equipment to wear at home. For those who need a breathing machine, the machine usually has to be bought, which is a considerable expense. The hard part is persistence: you must wear a mask every night. In the first weeks many people aren't used to it and take it off after a few hours.
- Plain language: Sleep apnea means breathing repeatedly stops or gets shallow during sleep, waking you and leaving you sleepy by day. With a CPAP machine, daytime sleepiness clearly drops, and car crashes fall by about 70%. Trials have not shown it prevents heart disease or extends life, so you use it to stay alert.
- Benefit: The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2017 diagnostic guideline describes who is more likely to have moderate to severe disease: marked daytime sleepiness on most days, plus at least two of the following three: (1) frequent loud snoring; (2) someone has seen you stop breathing, gasp or choke while asleep; (3) diagnosed hypertension. The guideline requires diagnosis by sleep monitoring, not questionnaires alone. Five or more pauses or shallow breaths per hour with symptoms counts as the condition; 15 or more counts regardless of symptoms.
  The AASM's 2019 systematic review pooled 38 randomized controlled trials. Compared with no treatment, people using a breathing machine cut their self-rated sleepiness score by 2.4 points (95% CI 1.9–2.8). This score is the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, out of 24, where higher means sleepier. Looking only at people who were not sleepy at baseline, 5 trials showed a 1.0-point drop, which the authors considered not clinically meaningful. The same review pooled 10 before-and-after studies. After starting treatment, the car crash rate was 0.3 times the pre-treatment rate (RR 0.3, 0.2–0.4), a reduction of about 70%. A 2010 analysis of 9 similar studies gave RR 0.278 (0.22–0.35). It also found that daytime sleepiness dropped clearly after one night of use, and simulated driving performance improved within 2 to 7 days. A 2009 analysis by the same group estimated that untreated patients had roughly 1.21 to 4.89 times the crash risk of others.
  Now the heart and lifespan. The SAVE trial enrolled 2,717 people aged 45 to 75 with moderate to severe disease who had coronary disease or had had a stroke. They were randomly assigned and followed for an average of 3.7 years. Cardiovascular events occurred in 17.0% of the breathing-machine group and 15.4% of the untreated group, with no significant difference (HR 1.10, 0.91–1.32). Users wore the machine an average of only 3.3 hours a night. Their snoring, daytime sleepiness, quality of life and mood improved. A 2017 analysis pooled 10 randomized trials with 7,266 people. Total mortality also showed no difference (RR 1.13, 0.99–1.29).
  A 2019 estimate says that about 176 million people aged 30 to 69 in China, or 23.6%, have this disease. Of those, about 65.52 million, or 8.8%, have moderate to severe disease, the largest number of any country in the world.
