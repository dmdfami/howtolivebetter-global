# 4. Don't Waste Time

This section counts only time. It asks how many hours you can save each day, week or year, or how much more often you finish a task on schedule. Time is not converted into lifespan or money here. Where money is involved, the section gives only the method, not a conclusion. Most evidence in this section comes from single studies or from national surveys on how people use their time, so the evidence levels are mostly B and C.

### 1. Write "I'll do it" as "at what time, in what place, and what I will do if something comes up"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes 1 to 2 minutes per task to write one "if... then..." sentence. For example: "Tuesday at 8 a.m., when I sit at my desk, I start by writing the introduction."
- Plain language: Rewrite "I plan to do this" as "Tuesday at 8 a.m., when I sit at my desk, I start by writing the introduction." Pooling 94 tests, people who write their plans this way reach their goals clearly more often. The effect is medium to large. Only the wording changes. It costs no money and little effort.
- Benefit: Pooling 94 independent tests, people who first write a plan as "if... then..." reach their goals more often. Psychologists call this an implementation intention. The effect size is d = 0.65, which is medium to large. Three steps help most: starting the task, holding firm when interrupted, and withdrawing from tasks that are bound to fail.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: This works only for things you really want to do. If you do not want the goal itself, writing more sentences will not help. Also, most of these studies used students and looked at health habits. The method may not work the same way at work.

### 2. Before starting, write down your stop conditions
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes 5 minutes at the start to write "If I have not reached a certain target by a certain date, I stop." The hard part is admitting it when that date comes. Stopping in front of others can feel embarrassing.
- Plain language: Before you start, write one sentence: if I have not reached a certain target by a certain date, I stop. Taken together, studies show this habit helps people step back from tasks that are bound to fail. What you save is all the time you would otherwise keep pouring into the task.
- Benefit: Pooling several implementation-intention studies, a stop rule set in advance also helps people withdraw from failing courses of action. The original term is "disengagement from failing courses of action." The time saved is the time you would otherwise have kept putting in.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Set the stop rule when you start, while you are still calm. If you set it afterward, the costs you have already spent will bias you. For the judgment itself, look only at future costs and future returns; see item 3. Also, "being willing to withdraw" is one sub-finding from the pooled studies. No one has measured separately how much time it saves.

### 3. When deciding whether to continue, look only at future costs and returns, not at what you have already spent
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is accepting that time already spent is gone and cannot be recovered.
- Plain language: Money and time you have already put in make you more likely to keep going. Pooling 98 groups of data, this tendency shows up consistently. So when you decide whether to continue, count only what you still have to put in and what you can still get back. Leave out what you have already spent.
- Benefit: Money, effort and time already invested make people more likely to keep going. Psychologists call this the sunk cost effect. Pooling 98 groups of data, the analysis confirms that this tendency is stable. Avoiding it means you do not keep putting in time just because you "don't want to waste" what you have already spent.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: This is how Arkes and Blumer's theater experiment worked. Ohio University's theater sold season tickets at random prices: full price of $15, or discounts of $2 or $7. In the first half of the season, the full-price group attended an average of 4.11 shows, versus 3.32 and 3.29 for the other two groups. In the second half, the three groups did not differ clearly. Roth et al. found that this effect fades over time, and older people are less affected by it.

### 4. When estimating how long a task will take, base the estimate on how long similar past tasks actually took, not on the plan
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes a few minutes before you estimate to check how long a similar task actually took last time.
- Plain language: Students estimated they would finish their theses in 33.9 days. They actually took 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished on the schedule they had estimated. In another group, people were first asked to recall how long a similar task took before estimating. The share finishing on time rose from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students expected to finish their theses in 33.9 days. They actually took 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished on the timeline they had estimated. Researchers asked another group to link their past experience to the current task before estimating. In this group, the share finishing on time rose from 29.3% in the control group to 60.0%. The optimism that makes people underestimate how long a job will take did not appear in this group.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: The Buehler study involved only Canadian university students and was a single study with a small sample: 37 people, and about 40 per group. Estimating from historical data on similar projects is called reference class forecasting. Flyvbjerg explains how to do it in engineering. Halkjelsvik reviewed many studies and found that underestimating is more common than overestimating. How large the gap is varies a lot by task and by research method.

### 5. Send an agenda before a meeting, and do not hold meetings without one; if you can stand, stand
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. The organizer spends 5 to 10 minutes writing an agenda before the meeting. Standing meetings require participants to cooperate.
- Plain language: Meetings that have an agenda sent in advance are more likely to be seen as useful by attendees. In one experiment, sitting meetings ran 34% longer than standing ones. The decisions made were no different in quality between the two formats.
- Benefit: Two surveys included 958 and 292 people. They found that meetings with an agenda sent in advance were more likely to be rated effective by attendees, and this link holds up statistically. In another experiment, 55 groups met sitting down and 56 met standing up. The sitting groups' meetings lasted 34% longer. The quality of the decisions made did not differ between the two formats.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>; Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Notes: The stand-up meeting experiment used tasks with five-person groups in a laboratory. Its conclusion cannot be applied directly to long meetings or large meetings. Leach measured how effective participants felt the meeting was, not how much work they actually got done.

### 6. Cut the number of meetings: if an issue can be settled in writing, do not hold a meeting
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. It requires colleagues and managers to cooperate. The hard part is that writing a clear message takes more effort than saying one sentence out loud.
- Plain language: The more meetings you attend in a day, the more tired you feel, and the more work you feel you have. If you cancel one meeting, the time you save is exactly the length of that meeting, with nothing lost.
- Benefit: Two surveys covered employees who worked 35 or more hours a week, with 676 and 304 people. How meetings affect work attitudes and well-being is not fixed. It depends on two things: how well meetings are run, and how much the job depends on other people. Another study had people keep a diary for a week. It found that the more meetings a person attended on a given day, the higher their fatigue and their sense of workload. The time saved equals the length of the meetings you cut.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>; Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Notes: Disputed: Rogelberg 2006 found that the link between the number of meetings and well-being is not one-directional. In jobs that depend heavily on other people, more meetings are not necessarily bad. Also, no study has directly compared switching to asynchronous written communication with holding meetings. This point is the authors' own inference.

### 7. Break a big task into subtasks before estimating and before starting
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. The breakdown itself takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- Plain language: When people estimate how long a job will take, they do not automatically split the task into parts and estimate each one. List the subtasks first, then estimate, and your estimate will be less likely to come out too short. Once a task is split, each small step can be started right away, so it is less likely to be put off.
- Benefit: When people estimate how long a job will take, they do not split a multi-part task into pieces on their own. Writing a literature review, writing the discussion, and organizing references are several separate jobs. Listing the subtasks before estimating reduces the habit of underestimating time. This habit is called the planning fallacy. Once split, each step becomes an action you can start right away, so procrastination also falls. Among the factors that most predict procrastination in studies are how aversive the task feels and whether it has been pushed back.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>; Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: A good level of detail is one where each subtask can be finished in one sitting. Splitting tasks too finely also takes time. Also, for the Kruger and Evans study, I verified only the direction of the finding from the abstract. I did not check the exact percentages against the full text.

### 8. For tasks with no outside deadline, set yourself a date
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is that once you set a date, you have to stick to it. If you ignore it when the day comes, it was not really set.
- Plain language: When another person sets the deadline, 80.6% of students finish before it. Only 38.7% finish within the time they themselves estimated. When a task gets done depends almost entirely on the deadline, and very little on the person's own estimate. So for a task with no deadline, set one yourself, and stick to it.
- Benefit: Among students with an external deadline, 80.6% finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within their own estimated time. When a task gets done depends almost entirely on the deadline, with a correlation of r = 0.82. The closer this number is to 1, the more tightly the two are linked. Their own estimates have only a weak link to it.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Notes: If you tell someone your self-set date, it becomes more like a deadline set by others. The research tested whether deadlines set by others work. Deadlines you set for yourself, such as time-boxing or the Pomodoro technique, have not been tested directly. This advice is an extrapolation from the research.

### 9. Treat procrastination as avoiding discomfort, not as a lack of effort
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Free. The only change is the direction of your thinking: first ask what about this task feels unpleasant, then look for a fix.
- Plain language: How unpleasant a task feels, whether you believe you can do it, how easily you are pulled away by something in front of you, and how much self-control you have are most strongly tied to procrastination. Traits such as neuroticism and sensation-seeking have only a weak link to it. So you can first make the task less unpleasant and make the first step smaller, rather than simply scolding yourself and forcing yourself through.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 691 correlation coefficients found that several factors have strong and stable links to procrastination: how aversive the task feels, how far away the deadline is, self-efficacy (believing you can do it), impulsiveness, and conscientiousness along with its facets of self-control, distractibility, orderliness and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking have only weak links to procrastination. The author uses temporal motivation theory to explain these results: the pull of a task drops quickly as its reward gets further away.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: The magnitude of benefit is rated "medium," because these studies only point to a direction and never measured how many hours could be saved. For specific actions, see item 1 (write "at what time, in what place, and what I will do if something comes up"), item 7 (break big tasks into subtasks), and item 8 (set a date for tasks with no outside deadline). These are summaries of correlational research, so they cannot show which factor causes which.

### 10. Keep what you need within reach, move what you don't want to touch farther away, and don't count on resisting in the moment
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Costs nothing and takes a few minutes. Put your phone in another room, and leave your books and computer out on the desk.
- Plain language: Things that are farther away get used less. Putting the same snacks farther away clearly reduces how much people eat, a medium-sized effect. Offering fewer options also clearly reduces how often people choose that food. These methods don't require you to resist on the spot, and you can set them up in a few minutes.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review of 24 randomized trials. Placing food farther away reduced how much was eaten, with a standardized mean difference of −0.60 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.36; this is the credible range; 12 studies, 1,098 people; low-certainty evidence), a medium effect. Reducing the options made the choice of that food drop more, with a standardized mean difference of −1.13 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.37; 3 studies, 154 people; low-certainty evidence). Regression analysis showed that the farther away the item and the fewer the options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Notes: The reason for grade B: all 24 studies in this review were about food. None covered alcohol or tobacco. 14 were lab studies, and all came from high-income countries. So applying "move it away to stop touching it" to your phone and games is an extrapolation, with no direct trial. One related idea has been tested directly: putting your phone out of sight. See section 3, item 1 (turn off non-essential notifications, and put your phone out of sight while working).

### 11. To force yourself to follow through, put money on the line, but first ask whether you will really stake it
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Deposit-style schemes require you to put up your own money first. If you don't follow through, the deposit is forfeited. The trial used a deposit of US$150.
- Plain language: To push yourself to follow through, you can get a reward once you've done it, or pay a deposit that is refunded when you do it, plus an added bonus. Among people willing to join either scheme, those who paid a deposit followed through 13 percentage points more often. But only 13.7% were willing to pay a deposit, while 90% would accept a reward. So deposits only work for people who are willing to pay one.
- Benefit: A US trial of 2,538 people. Employees and their family members or friends were randomly assigned to one of four incentive programs or to usual care. The acceptance rate was 90.0% for the reward program (about US$800 for success) and 13.7% for the deposit program (US$150 paid up front, plus a US$650 bonus). Six-month sustained quit rates were 9.4% to 16.0% across the four incentive programs, and 6.0% for usual care. The reward program had 15.7% and the deposit program 10.2%. But among only the 13.7% who accepted both, the six-month quit rate in the deposit group was 13.2 percentage points higher than in the reward group (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8; this is the credible range).
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Notes: This trial tested quitting smoking. Applying it to writing a paper or exercising is an extrapolation, so the size of the benefit is rated "medium." When choosing a scheme, first check whether you will actually use it, not which one works best. Many people won't accept an approach they dislike, however strong its effect, so it does nothing for them. Pick the one you are willing to accept. Even a small deposit is better than none. For how to quit smoking itself, see section 2, item 3 (quit-smoking medication).

### 12. Count new habits in months, not "21 days"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. What it takes is doing the same action every day in the same situation, for several months.
- Plain language: The claim that a habit forms in 21 days has no basis. In one study that tracked people for 12 weeks, the time for an action to become automatic ranged from 18 days to 254 days. Still finding it hard after two or three weeks is normal. Missing one day in between doesn't reset your progress.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each chose one action involving eating, drinking or physical activity. They did it every day in a fixed situation (for example, "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, rating each day how automatic it felt. Data from 82 people were enough for analysis, and the model fit 62 of them. The number of days needed to reach 95% of each person's automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 to 254. The study also found that missing one opportunity to perform the action had no substantial effect on how the habit formed.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Notes: This is the only study, with 96 people, and the actions were simple (for example, eating a piece of fruit after a meal). How long complex actions take, such as running three times a week or writing every day, has not been tested. So still finding it hard after two weeks is not evidence of failure. To make the repetition actually happen, tie the action to a fixed situation. See item 1 of this section (write it as "at what time, where, and what to do when X happens").

### 13. If procrastination is already affecting your life, use self-help materials backed by randomized trials instead of looking for a therapist
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: A self-help book costs a few dozen yuan, and some are free. It takes about ten weeks, reading one chapter and doing one exercise each week.
- Plain language: Procrastination that causes real distress can improve. Working through cognitive behavioral self-help materials for ten weeks helps, whether a therapist guides you or you do it entirely on your own. Both are clearly better than doing nothing. Guided and unguided groups showed no difference, so working through the materials yourself also works.
- Benefit: 150 people were randomly divided into three groups: guided self-help with a therapist, unguided self-help, and a waitlist control. After ten weeks, the between-group effect sizes on the procrastination scale were d = 0.70 for the guided group (95% CI 0.29–1.10; this is the credible range) and d = 0.50 for the unguided group (0.10–0.90). On the irrational procrastination scale they were 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09), respectively. The proportion reaching a clinically meaningful improvement was 31.3% to 40.0% in the guided group and 24.0% to 36.0% in the unguided group. The two groups did not differ significantly on any outcome.
- Evidence level: A
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Notes: China only: The participants were Swedish adults, and the materials were in Swedish. There is no identical set in Chinese, so the size of the benefit is rated "medium." The main components are the usual ones: behavioral activation, behavioral experiments, stimulus control, and explanations about motivation and work methods. Stimulus control is item 10 of this section (move away what you don't want to touch). If procrastination comes with clear low mood or anxiety, first follow section 3, item 18 (get moving, get sunlight, sleep on time, talk to someone, and call 12356, China's national mental health hotline).

### 14. Use your hourly wage to decide which chores to outsource
<!-- tags: money=high time=low will=no gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Outsourcing costs money, and the amount depends on the service you buy. You also need to spend some time first working out your hourly wage.
- Plain language: On average, Chinese people spend 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework. Among those who actually did housework that day, the figure is 1 hour 59 minutes. People who pay to buy time report higher life satisfaction. For the same amount of money, spending it on buying time makes people happier in the moment than spending it on buying things.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework. Counting only those who did housework, the average is 1 hour 59 minutes. This comes from a 2024 survey covering 38,500 households and 107,000 people. Another study sampled 6,271 people across four countries, and people who paid to buy time reported higher life satisfaction. The researchers also ran experiments in real life. For the same amount of money, spending it on buying time led to greater happiness in the moment than spending it on physical goods.
- Evidence level: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>；国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes: First work out your hourly rate: monthly after-tax income ÷ actual hours worked per month. Then check how much this chore costs to outsource. If the outsourcing price is below your hourly rate, and you would really use the saved time to earn money or rest, outsource it. Everyone's hourly rate and outsourcing prices differ, so work out the conclusion for yourself. Also note that Whillans measured whether people were happier after buying time, not how many hours they saved.

### 15. Set a hard daily limit on short videos and aimless scrolling
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=high gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The actions take only a few minutes: turn off push notifications, delete the app, or set a daily time limit on the app. The hard part is holding back and not opening it.
- Plain language: In 2024, Chinese people spent an average of 5 hours 37 minutes online per day. Among those who were online that day, the figure was 6 hours 3 minutes. Using the same method, the 2018 figure was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest number in this section. Cutting one hour a day adds 365 hours a year.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 5 hours 37 minutes online per day. Counting only those who went online that day, the average is 6 hours 3 minutes. 92.9% of people went online that day. These are 2024 figures. Using the same method, the 2018 figure was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest number in this section. Cutting one hour from it each day equals 365 extra hours per year. Separately, according to the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC), by June 2025 netizens spent an average of 30.6 hours per week online, 1.9 hours more than six months earlier. There were 1.068 billion short-video users, 95.1% of netizens.
- Evidence level: C
- Sources: 国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>；国家统计局 (2019). 2018年全国时间利用调查公报. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>；中国互联网络信息中心 (2025). 第 56 次《中国互联网络发展状况统计报告》. <https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2025/0730/MAIN1753846666507QEK67ZS9DH.pdf>
- Notes: Online time includes work, study and socializing, so not all of it is wasted. It is graded C because no study has measured how much time people got back after limiting their use, or where that time went. Only total statistics are available. For the category of "looking at how others are doing," a randomized trial has measured how much time was saved and how mood changed. See section 3, item 20.

### 16. Stop watching TV and scrolling through news. Check the information you need at set times
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some gain=high scope=time -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The difficulty is breaking the habit of turning things on without thinking, and you might miss some instant messages.
- Plain language: Americans aged 15 and over watch 2.6 hours of TV a day, which takes up half of all their free time. In 2018, Chinese people averaged 1 hour 40 minutes of TV a day, and people aged 75 to 84 watched 3 hours 16 minutes. If you switch to checking the information you need at set times, the time you save comes from this same block.
- Benefit: Americans aged 15 and over watch TV for 2.6 hours a day. Their total leisure time is 5.2 hours, so TV takes up half of it. This is the 2025 figure. Chinese residents watch TV an average of 1 hour 40 minutes a day, and people aged 75 to 84 watch 3 hours 16 minutes. This is the 2018 figure.
- Evidence level: C
- Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey — 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm>；国家统计局 (2019). 2018年全国时间利用调查公报. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>
- Notes: Only total statistics are available. No study has measured where the time went after people cut back on TV and news. The "check at set times" approach comes from the author's own experience.

### 17. Spend time learning keyboard shortcuts and automation for the software you use often
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some gain=mid scope=time -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Learning takes a one-time investment of several hours to several dozen hours. The difficulty is that at first it is slower than using the mouse.
- Plain language: Most people who have used Word for years still click toolbar icons. In a controlled comparison experiment, keyboard shortcuts were faster than both menus and icons. Learning them is a one-time cost. After that, every use each day pays back the cost. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more you gain.
- Benefit: The study looked at 251 experienced Word users. Most of them rarely used the faster keyboard shortcuts and mainly clicked toolbar icons. A comparison experiment confirmed that among menus, icons and keyboard shortcuts, keyboard shortcuts were the fastest. The time saved builds up a little with each day's operations. People who do the same kind of work over a long period gain the most.
- Evidence level: B

### 18. When choosing housing, put commute time first to shorten your one-way commute

- Cost: High. You may have to pay higher rent or move to a smaller home. Moving itself also takes time.
- Plain language: In German long-term tracking data, people with longer commutes felt their lives were worse. This loss was not made up for by higher wages or better housing. Cutting 30 minutes from each one-way trip saves about 5 hours a week.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 50 minutes a day on travel. Counting only those who traveled that day, the average is 1 hour 2 minutes. These are 2024 figures.
