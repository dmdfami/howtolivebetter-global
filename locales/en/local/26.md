### 1. Don't collect money and pass it on to sellers yourself; have payments settled directly through a licensed payment institution
- In the United States: Platforms that accept money from buyers and hold it before paying sellers generally fall under the definition of a money transmitter. You must register with FinCEN at the federal level and obtain money transmitter licenses in almost every state where your users live. Operating without a license can result in civil penalties and federal criminal prosecution. To avoid this, platforms typically use a payment processor like Stripe Connect or PayPal to handle the money directly.
- Official sources: Title 18, United States Code, Section 1960. https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section1960&num=0&edition=prelim ; Title 31, Code of Federal Regulations, Section 1010.100. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-A/section-1010.100

### 5. If you let users sell on your platform, you must verify and register sellers, report their information, and keep records for three years
- In the United States: The INFORM Consumers Act requires online marketplaces to collect, verify, and disclose the identity of high-volume third-party sellers (those with 200 or more sales and $5,000 or more in revenue in 12 months). You must collect their bank account, tax ID, and contact details, and verify this information annually. You also must provide a reporting mechanism for buyers on the seller's product pages. Violations can result in civil penalties of over $50,000 per violation from the FTC.
- Official sources: Title 15, United States Code, Section 45f (INFORM Consumers Act). https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title15-section45f&num=0&edition=prelim
- Checked on: 2026-11-02
- Reliability: verified

### 6. You must manage what users post: review mechanisms, a visible report button, and immediate takedown and reporting of illegal content
- In the United States: Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act generally protects platforms from being held legally responsible for what users post. You are not required by federal law to proactively monitor or review all user content for illegal material before it goes live. However, you can choose to moderate content and remove posts that violate your terms of service without losing this legal protection.
- Official sources: Title 47, United States Code, Section 230. https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title47-section230&num=0&edition=prelim

### 8. Do not open live-streaming accounts for minors under 16, and handle tips by age
- In the United States: The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) requires you to obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children under 13, not 16. There is no federal law specifically banning children from opening live-streaming accounts or setting age tiers for tipping, though platforms usually ban children under 13 to avoid COPPA compliance burdens. State laws regarding contracts with minors may allow a minor to void a transaction and demand a refund for tips.
- Official sources: Title 15, United States Code, Sections 6501-6506 (COPPA). https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title15/chapter91&edition=prelim

### 9. Act promptly on infringement notices, and restore content if no complaint or lawsuit follows 15 days after the seller's rebuttal is forwarded
- In the United States: The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) provides a safe harbor if you promptly remove copyrighted content upon receiving a valid takedown notice. If the user submits a valid counter-notice, you must forward it to the person who complained. You must then restore the content in 10 to 14 business days, unless the complainant notifies you that they have filed a lawsuit against the user.
- Official sources: Title 17, United States Code, Section 512. https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title17-section512&num=0&edition=prelim

### 13. If you sell auto-renewing memberships, remind users clearly before sign-up and before each charge, and make cancellation easy to find
- In the United States: The federal Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) requires you to clearly disclose all material terms of an auto-renewing transaction before obtaining billing information and to provide a simple mechanism to stop recurring charges. Many states, such as California, have even stricter laws requiring explicit consent, reminder notices before renewal, and immediate online cancellation options. The FTC actively enforces these rules and can seek civil penalties for making cancellation difficult.
- Official sources: Title 15, United States Code, Sections 8401-8403 (ROSCA). https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title15/chapter110&edition=prelim ; Title 16, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 425. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-425
