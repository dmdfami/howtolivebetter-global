### 1. Before taking two cold or pain medicines together, check the ingredient list: acetaminophen should appear in only one of them, and if you take too much, go to hospital the same day even if you feel fine
- In the United States: Acetaminophen is the common name for this drug. The FDA requires OTC combination products to carry a liver warning stating that severe liver damage may occur if you take it with other drugs containing acetaminophen. In case of overdose, get medical help or contact a Poison Control Center (1-800-222-1222) right away, even if you do not notice any signs or symptoms.
- Official sources: 21 CFR 201.326(a)(1)(iii)(A) Liver warning <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>; MedlinePlus Acetaminophen <https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a681004.html>

- Reliability: verified

### 2. Do not give children aspirin, analgin (metamizole) or nimesulide, do not give analgin-containing cold medicine to anyone under 18, and measure liquid fever medicine with the dosing device packed in the box
- In the United States: Neither analgin (metamizole) nor nimesulide are FDA-approved, so they are not legally available for anyone. The FDA requires aspirin labels to warn that children and teenagers recovering from chicken pox or flu-like symptoms should not use it, due to the risk of Reye's syndrome. For measuring liquid medicine, the FDA advises parents to use the dosing device that comes with the medicine, not household spoons.
- Official sources: 21 CFR 201.314(h)(1) Reye's syndrome <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.314>; U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Questions and Answers – Important change in concentration for over-the-counter (OTC) liquid acetaminophen marketed for infants <https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/questions-and-answers-important-change-concentration-over-counter-otc-liquid-acetaminophen-marketed>
- Checked on: 2026-11-02
- Reliability: verified

### 4. For children under 2 with a cold, do not give combination cold medicines or cough medicines on your own
- In the United States: The FDA states that OTC cough and cold products should not be given to infants and children under 2 years of age because they can cause serious and potentially life-threatening side effects. Furthermore, manufacturers voluntarily label these OTC products to state "Do not use in children under 4 years of age."
- Official sources: U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Should You Give Kids Medicine for Coughs and Colds? <https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/when-give-kids-medicine-coughs-and-colds>
- Checked on: 2026-11-02
- Reliability: verified

### 6. For heartburn, buy omeprazole yourself for no more than 7 days; for trouble swallowing, vomiting blood or black stools, go straight to hospital
- In the United States: OTC omeprazole labels state it can be taken for up to 14 days, and you should not take more than one course every 4 months. You should not use it if you have trouble swallowing, vomit blood, or have bloody or black stools, as these may be signs of a serious condition. The FDA label for clopidogrel (Plavix) states to avoid taking it with omeprazole, because omeprazole significantly reduces its effect whether taken together or 12 hours apart.
- Official sources: U.S. Food and Drug Administration. ### 7. Don't ask a doctor for antibiotics for a common cold
- In the United States: Antibiotics are prescription-only medications. MedlinePlus states that antibiotics should not be used to treat a common cold, because they do not work on viral infections.
- Official sources: MedlinePlus. Common cold <https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000678.htm>; MedlinePlus. Antibiotic Resistance <https://medlineplus.gov/antibioticresistance.html>

### 10. Regularly clear out expired and unused medicines, and put them in the hazardous waste bin with their packaging rather than keeping them at home
- In the United States: The FDA says the best way to dispose of unused or expired medicines is to find a drug take-back location or use a prepaid mail-back envelope. If these are not available, you can flush medicines that are on the FDA's Flush List. For other medicines, the FDA recommends mixing them with dirt, cat litter, or used coffee grounds, sealing the mixture in a plastic bag, and throwing it in the household trash.
- Official sources: U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Disposal of Unused Medicines: What You Should Know <https://www.fda.gov/drugs/safe-disposal-medicines/disposal-unused-medicines-what-you-should-know>
- Checked on: 2026-11-02
- Reliability: verified

### 12. If you take a cold or antihistamine medicine containing chlorphenamine (chlorpheniramine) or diphenhydramine, do not drive, work at heights or operate machinery while taking it
- In the United States: The FDA states that cold remedies and allergy medicines containing antihistamines like chlorpheniramine or diphenhydramine can cause drowsiness. For medicines containing these ingredients, the FDA requires labels to warn consumers to use caution when driving a motor vehicle or operating machinery.
- Official sources: U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Some Medicines and Driving Don't Mix <https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/some-medicines-and-driving-dont-mix>; 21 CFR 341.72(c) <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-341/subpart-C/section-341.72>
